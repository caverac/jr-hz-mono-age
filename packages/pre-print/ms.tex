\title{The Thickness of Mono-age Disk Populations Follows the Curvature of the Total Potential}

\author{Carlos Vera-Ciro}
\affiliation{Independent Researcher}
\email{caverac@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
We test two models that relate the thickness of a mono-age disk population to its vertical
velocity dispersion $\sigz$: an isothermal tracer of the total potential,
$\rho \propto \exp(-\dPhi/\sigz^2)$, and a self-gravitating sheet, $z_0 = \sigz^2/(\pi G
\Sigma)$, whose thickness grows as $\sigz^2$. In seven Auriga disks, the tracer predicts the scale heights of \tracerWithinTen\ of
\populations\ populations within 10 percent at every age, nearly as well as the Jeans equation,
whereas the sheet does so for \sheetWithinTen, predicting the youngest populations \sheetYoung\
times as thick as measured and the oldest \sheetOld\ times. Although the two models coincide for
a self-gravitating layer, each population holds a small part of the mass of its disk and moves
in the potential of all of it, so its height grows as $\sigz^\eta$, with $\eta$ of
\etaRange\ set by the curvature of that potential, which makes age-thickness relations grow as
$\mathrm{age}^{\eta\beta_z}$ rather than $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$ for $\sigz \propto \mathrm{age}^{\beta_z}$.
Their dispersions rise with height. Summed, the tracers
reproduce the heights and flares of the whole disks. Near the Sun, Gaia DR3 star
counts split by LAMOST and APOGEE ages follow the tracer with no trend with age over 0-10\,Gyr, match its
level in potentials with the measured local density, and imply midplane densities of
\inversionRhoZero\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ from LAMOST and two APOGEE age scales, consistent with the measured $0.097 \pm 0.013$.
The heights of Imig et al. (2025) agree with our counts in level but grow more slowly with age.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The vertical structure of a population of stars of one age records how the disk heated it.
Both its dispersion \citep{Holmberg2009,TingRix2019,Sharma2021} and its thickness grow with
age, the thickness now measured population by population, split by abundance or by age, in
large spectroscopic and astrometric surveys \citep{Bovy2012b,Bovy2016,Mackereth2017,Mirabal2026}. Turning one
into the other needs a model of the vertical structure, of which two are in use. In the first, a
population of one age is an isothermal tracer of the total gravitational potential, whose
density falls as $\exp[-\dPhi(R,z)/\sigz^2]$ with the vertical potential $\dPhi$ of all the
mass. Models of the Milky Way disk have long been built this way, from a sum of such
populations with dispersions prescribed by an age-velocity relation and compared with star
counts that mix all ages
\citep{Bienayme1987,Robin2003,JustJahreiss2010,SysoliatinaJust2021,SysoliatinaJust2022}.
The density of a single tracer has been predicted from its kinematics in this way
\citep{Bahcall1984,HolmbergFlynn2000}. The inverse problem, inferring the potential near the
Sun from the density and the kinematics of a tracer, goes back to \citet{Kapteyn1922} and \citet{Oort1932,Oort1960}
and was taken up with K dwarfs toward the south Galactic pole by
\citet{KuijkenGilmore1989a,KuijkenGilmore1989b}. Mono-abundance populations, whose vertical
dispersions change little with height \citep{Bovy2012}, have been used in the same direction
\citep{BovyRix2013}.

In the second model, a population is a self-gravitating isothermal sheet of surface density
$\Sigma$, with the profile $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ and $z_0 = \sigz^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$
\citep{Spitzer1942,vanderKruitSearle1981}. The sheet is the tracer of a layer whose own mass
makes its potential, which is how it describes the old disk as a whole. Studies of disk heating
and radial migration have adopted it for single populations as well, mostly as a scaling between
dispersion and thickness \citep{Roskar2013,MartigMinchevFlynn2014b,Grand2016,Mackereth2019}.
\citet[Sect.~5.3]{Martig2026}, for example, use it to turn a dispersion that grows as
$\mathrm{age}^{\beta_z}$ into an expected thickness that grows as $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$.
The two models agree for a population that makes its own potential, and differ for one that
holds a small part of the mass of its disk. At fixed $\Sigma$ the sheet makes every
population grow as $\sigz^2$, whereas a tracer grows as $\sigz^\eta$, with $\eta$ set by
the shape of $\dPhi$ over the heights that the population reaches: $\eta = 1$ where the
potential is harmonic and $\eta = 2$ where it grows linearly with height
\citep{vanderKruit1988}. \citet{Ludlow2021} interpolate between these limits to predict the half-mass heights of
idealized single-component disks from their dispersions. \citet{BenitezLlambay2018} find the same
transition in the thickness of gaseous disks. To our knowledge, neither model has been tested quantitatively, population by
population, against the heights of mono-age populations measured independently of it. The models of the Milky Way are fitted to
counts that mix the populations, with dispersions from an age-velocity relation, whereas the
inverse problem takes the heights as data rather than predicting them.

The question also bears on the relation between radial action and scale height, which
\citet{Jia2023} find in the Milky Way, \citet{Jia2024} recover in an $N$-body disk, and
\citet{Palicio2025} find across 23 simulated disks, seven of them from Auriga. \citet{Jia2023}
read the relation through the radial action of an epicycle and the thickness of a layer at fixed
midplane density. Its slope has been derived for a whole disk from the radial action of an
epicycle and the scale height of a sheet (C.~Vera-Ciro, in press), so that the age dependence of
the slope for a single population depends on which vertical model holds.

Here we test the tracer and the sheet population by population. In seven disks of the Auriga
simulations \citep{Grand2017,Grand2024}, whose heights we measure from the star particles and
whose potentials we fit to all the particles, we predict the scale height of every
population of 1\,Gyr of ages from its own dispersion, and compare the predictions with the
measured heights, with the vertical Jeans equation, which assumes nothing about the kinematics,
as the reference for any such test. We
measure how the heights grow with the dispersion, and compare that growth with the local
slope of the tracer of each potential. In the solar neighborhood, we split star counts of
Gaia giants into populations with the ages of LAMOST and APOGEE, and test the tracer in a
family of potentials that match the measured density near the Sun. We describe the
vertical structure of a tracer and our estimator in Section~\ref{sec:tracer}, the Auriga
populations in Section~\ref{sec:auriga}, whole disks in Section~\ref{sec:whole}, the Milky Way
in Sections~\ref{sec:milkyway} and~\ref{sec:imig}, and discuss the consequences in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{The vertical structure of a tracer}
\label{sec:tracer}

\subsection{The tracer and the sheet}

A population whose distribution function depends on the vertical energy as
$\exp(-E_z/\sigz^2)$ has, in the vertical potential $\dPhi(z) = \Phi(R,z) - \Phi(R,0)$ at a
radius $R$, the density
\begin{equation}
\rho(z) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{\dPhi(z)}{\sigz^2}\right],
\label{eq:tracer}
\end{equation}
with the same dispersion $\sigz$ at every height. We call this population an isothermal
tracer, and take $\dPhi$ from the potential of all the mass. When the population makes the
potential itself, the Poisson equation closes Eq.~(\ref{eq:tracer}) into the sheet of
\citet{Spitzer1942},
\begin{equation}
\rho(z) \propto \mathrm{sech}^2\left(\frac{z}{z_0}\right), \qquad
z_0 = \frac{\sigz^2}{\pi G \Sigma}.
\label{eq:sheet}
\end{equation}
Above $z_0$ the sheet falls as an exponential of scale $z_0/2$, which is why the relation is
also written $\sigz^2 = 2\pi G \Sigma h$ for the scale $h$ of that tail. Because the two
scales differ by a factor of 2, we take neither by convention: our estimator
(Section~\ref{sec:estimator}) returns $z_0$ within 0.3 percent when applied to a sheet, so a
population that is a sheet would have $\hZ = z_0$, which is the height the sheet predicts. Applied
to a population of one age, $\Sigma$ is the surface density of all the mass near the plane,
which we take within $|z| < 1.1\kpc$, so that the sheet predicts $\hZ \propto \sigz^2$ for
every population of a disk.

\subsection{The vertical Jeans equation}
\label{sec:jeanseq}

The tracer solves the vertical Jeans equation of a steady axisymmetric population of density
$\nu$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial(\nu\sigz^2)}{\partial z} + \frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial(R\nu\sigma_{Rz}^2)}{\partial R}
= -\nu\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z},
\label{eq:jeans}
\end{equation}
under two assumptions: that $\sigz$ does not change with height, and that the second term, the
tilt term, vanishes, where $\sigma_{Rz}^2 \equiv \langle v_R v_z\rangle$ is the covariance of the
radial and vertical velocities. Writing the tilt term as $\nu t(z)$, with
$t(z) = (R\nu)^{-1}\,\partial(R\nu\sigma_{Rz}^2)/\partial R$ an acceleration that adds to the
vertical force, Eq.~(\ref{eq:jeans}) integrates to
\begin{equation}
\nu(z) = \frac{\sigz^2(0)}{\sigz^2(z)}\exp\left[-\int_0^z
\frac{\partial\Phi/\partial z' + t(z')}{\sigz^2(z')}\,dz'\right],
\label{eq:jeansdensity}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~(\ref{eq:tracer}) when $\sigz$ is constant and $t = 0$. With $\sigz(z)$ and $t(z)$
measured at every height, Eq.~(\ref{eq:jeansdensity}) assumes nothing about the distribution
function beyond a steady state and axisymmetry, so that its scatter about the measured heights is
the reference for any prediction from the kinematics, although it also carries the noise of the
dispersion and the tilt term measured in every slice. In $N$-body disks, a dispersion that changes
with height improves estimates of the local density from one tracer \citep{Garbari2011}, as does
the tilt term \citep{Sivertsson2022}. The tracer (Eq.~\ref{eq:tracer}), the sheet
(Eq.~\ref{eq:sheet}) and the Jeans equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:jeansdensity}) are the three
predictions that we set against the measured heights.

\subsection{The local slope of the heights}

For a tracer in a potential $\dPhi \propto |z|^p$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:tracer}) gives a height that
grows as $\sigz^{2/p}$. We define the local slope
\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{d\ln \hZ}{d\ln\sigz},
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
which is 1 in a harmonic potential, as near the midplane of any smooth distribution of mass,
and 2 in a potential that grows linearly with height, as above a thin one
\citep{vanderKruit1988}. A real disk has a harmonic core and a linear rise, so $\eta$ grows
with $\sigz$ from 1 toward 2 as the populations reach heights where the potential is no
longer harmonic. If $\sigz \propto \mathrm{age}^{\beta_z}$, Eq.~(\ref{eq:eta}) gives
$d\ln\hZ/d\ln\mathrm{age} = \eta\beta_z$, so that a tracer thickens as
$\mathrm{age}^{\eta\beta_z}$ over a range of $\sigz$ in which $\eta$ is
constant, whereas the sheet at fixed $\Sigma$ gives $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$. At heights
comparable to the radius, where the vertical force falls with height, $\dPhi$ grows more slowly
than $|z|$, so $\eta$ would
exceed 2 there. In the fitted potentials of the Auriga disks, the local slope of the tracer is at
most \curveEtaMax\ at the dispersions from 5 to 150\kms\ at which we measure it
(Section~\ref{sec:eta}).

\subsection{What a test of the tracer can show}

Part of any agreement between the tracer and the measured heights is guaranteed. In a
harmonic potential $\dPhi = \Omega_z^2 z^2/2$, with vertical frequency $\Omega_z$, the vertical
virial theorem of a steady population gives $\langle v_z^2\rangle = \Omega_z^2 \langle z^2\rangle$
whatever its distribution function, so the rms height of a cold population whose dispersion near
the plane represents its stars follows from its vertical velocities as Eq.~(\ref{eq:tracer})
gives it. Its windowed scale height also depends on the shape of its profile: populations whose
distribution functions of the vertical energy are $\exp[-(E_z/E_0)^q]$ with $q$ of 0.7 and 1.5
have 0.85 and 1.14 times the height of the isothermal one with the same $\langle v_z^2\rangle$,
at every dispersion. The hot populations, which reach heights where the potential is no longer
harmonic, depend on both the shape and the potential. We therefore report the cold and the hot
populations separately.

\subsection{The scale height and its window}
\label{sec:estimator}

We measure the scale height of a set of heights $|z|$, or of a profile, by maximum
likelihood of an exponential truncated at a window $L$. Its mean height inside the window is
\begin{equation}
\langle z\rangle = h - \frac{L}{e^{L/h} - 1},
\label{eq:truncated}
\end{equation}
which we solve for $h$. The maximum-likelihood estimate of the rate $1/h$ is positive only when
$\langle z\rangle < L/2$ \citep[Sect.~3]{DeemerVotaw1955}, so a window whose mean reaches
$L/2$ gives no height. As \citet{Palicio2025} do, we average the estimates within $L$ and
within $L/2$. A profile that is not exponential has no single scale, so its fitted height
depends on the window: a window narrower than the profile reads its flat core as a large
scale (Appendix~\ref{app:window}). We therefore scale the window with the population,
$L = 2\hZ$, taking the smallest $L$ that satisfies this condition, so that populations of
one profile shape are fitted over the same part of their profiles. An exponential returns
its scale in any such window, whereas the sheet of Eq.~(\ref{eq:sheet}) returns $z_0$ within 0.3
percent. A window needs 100 stars inside $L/2$: with fewer, the profile of a
hot population is nearly flat inside a small window, so that the noise of the fitted $1/h$
can satisfy the condition by chance far below the true scale (Appendix~\ref{app:window}).
Every prediction but the sheet, whose $z_0$ the estimator returns within 0.3 percent, passes
through the same estimator as the stars. Because no window is free
of convention, we also measure the half-mass height, below which half of the mass within
$|z| < 12\kpc$ lies.

\section{Mono-age populations in Auriga}
\label{sec:auriga}

\subsection{Simulations and measurements}
\label{sec:measurements}

We use the seven halos of the Auriga simulations \citep{Grand2017} at the standard resolution
of the public release \citep{Grand2024} that \citet[Table~1]{Palicio2025} analyze, Au2, Au3,
Au16, Au19, Au23, Au24 and Au27, at redshift zero. For each halo we fit an axisymmetric
potential to all the particles of the snapshot with AGAMA \citep{Vasiliev2019}, as a
CylSpline expansion for the gas and the stars of the disk region and a Multipole expansion for
everything else. The star particles of the annulus $7.5 < R < 9\kpc$ are split into ten bins of
1\,Gyr in age, from 0 to 10\,Gyr, which give \populations\ populations. In each bin, $\sigz$ is the
dispersion of $v_z$ of the stars with $|z| < 0.3\kpc$, clipped at four standard deviations
estimated from the median absolute deviation, whereas $\hZ$ is the scale height of the heights of
all its stars, weighted by their initial mass, in the window of Section~\ref{sec:estimator}.
The tracer of Eq.~(\ref{eq:tracer}) takes $\dPhi$ from the fitted potential at the median
radius of the annulus, whereas the sheet of Eq.~(\ref{eq:sheet}) takes $\Sigma$ from all the
particles of the annulus within $|z| < 1.1\kpc$. Errors are half the range between the 16th
and the 84th percentiles of 300 bootstrap resamplings of the stars of each bin, each of which
recomputes every quantity, so that the ratio of a predicted to the measured height carries the
correlation of the two. A prediction agrees with a measured height within $x$ percent when
$|\ln h_\mathrm{pred}/\hZ| \le x/100$, the band that the figures draw. Every percentage that compares two heights in this work is such
a logarithm. The scatter of a set of predictions is the root mean square of
$\ln h_\mathrm{pred}/\hZ$. On a mock snapshot of a
known galaxy, the analysis recovers $\dPhi$
within \mockPhi\ and the height of every bin within \mockHz\ (Appendix~\ref{app:mock}). In
the real snapshots, the potential averaged over azimuth gives tracer heights \snapshotRange\
times those of the fit.

\subsection{The local slope of the heights}
\label{sec:eta}

We fit $\eta$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:eta}) as the slope of the weighted least-squares line of
$\ln\hZ$ against $\ln\sigz$ across the bins of each halo. Both coordinates carry errors, so each
bin is weighted by the inverse of $(\delta\hZ/\hZ)^2 + \eta^2(\delta\sigz/\sigz)^2$, refitted
until $\eta$ converges. The error is scaled by $\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$ where $\chi^2_\nu > 1$.
Over 0-10\,Gyr, $\eta$ spans \etaRange\ across the seven halos (circles in
Figure~\ref{fig:eta}), with errors of \etaErrRange,
between the value of 1 of a harmonic potential and the value of 2 of a linear one. Because
$\eta$ is a local slope, it changes with the range of ages, to \etaAllRanges\ over the ranges
0-10, 2-10, 0-8 and 2-8\,Gyr.

Figure~\ref{fig:eta} sets the fitted $\eta$ against the local slope of the tracer itself (gray
lines), which the curvature of each potential fixes. For each halo we compute the
tracer of the fitted potential at 41 dispersions from 5 to 150\kms, measure its height in the
window, and take the logarithmic derivative. The local slope rises from \curveEtaAtTen\ at
10\kms, where the populations stay inside the harmonic core of the potential, to
\curveEtaAtForty\ at 40\kms, and to at most \curveEtaMax\ at the hottest dispersions. Over the
dispersions of the populations of each halo, the measured $\eta$ exceeds the slope of the chord
of this curve by \etaChordDiff, within two standard errors in \etaChordWithinTwo\ halos.
The exception is Au19, whose $\eta$ of \auNineteenEta\ exceeds the chord of
\auNineteenChord, because its old populations are thicker than the tracer predicts
(Section~\ref{sec:jeans}). The slope stays below 2 over the whole grid: in \curveNoScaleCount\
halos the window finds no scale from \curveNoScale\kms\ on, where $2\hZ$ of the tracer would
exceed the 12\kpc\ of the grid of heights. The hottest population of each of these halos
lies a factor of at least \noScaleMargin\ below the last dispersion at which the window finds a
scale. The heights of the populations
therefore grow with their dispersions as the curvature of each potential prescribes.

\subsection{Age dependence and the Jeans equation}
\label{sec:jeans}

The age dependence of the heights tests the tracer more strictly than its scatter does,
because a prediction can scatter about the measured heights without a trend, or follow them in
scatter but miss how they change with age. We fit the trend $p$ of $h_\mathrm{pred}/\hZ \propto
\mathrm{age}^p$ by weighted least squares over the bins of each halo, with the error scaled by
$\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$. A prediction that reproduces how $\hZ$ changes with age has $p = 0$. Over
0-10\,Gyr the trend of the tracer lies within \trendMaxZero\ standard errors of 0 in every halo,
with values of \trendRangeZero, so that its ratios in the top panel of Figure~\ref{fig:trends}
do not drift with age. Over 2-10\,Gyr it is negative in \trendNegativeTwo\ halos, a split of
signs with a probability of \trendSignTestTwo\ under a scatter symmetric about 0.
Figure~\ref{fig:checks} shows these trends, one row per halo (filled circles), next to those of
the checks of Sections~\ref{sec:jeans} to \ref{sec:robustness}, which ask where the residual
comes from. The ratio of the tracer to the measured heights therefore declines slightly with
age, from a median over the halos of \tracerMedianMid\ at 2-6\,Gyr to \tracerMedianOld\ at
8-10\,Gyr, by an amount that only some halos resolve on their own.

The vertical Jeans equation tests where that residual comes from. The dispersion rises with
height in \sigmaRisePopulations\ populations: at twice the measured height, the dispersion of
all the stars of a slice of $|z|$ over that of the slice nearest the plane has a median of
\sigmaRiseTwiceHeight. With the variance of $v_z$ of each population measured in slices of $|z|$ up to 12\kpc,
without clipping, the Jeans equation gives heights within 10 percent in
\jeansSigmaWithinTen\ populations. Its trends over 2-10\,Gyr are negative in
\jeansSigmaNegativeTwo\ halos (filled diamonds in Figure~\ref{fig:checks}), with a mean over the
halos, weighted by their inverse variances, of \jeansSigmaTrendMeanTwo\ against
\tracerTrendMeanTwo\ for the tracer, so the pattern of signs of the tracer is gone. In
Eq.~(\ref{eq:jeansdensity}), the prefactor $\sigz^2(0)/\sigz^2(z)$ lowers the density wherever the
dispersion has risen. The rising dispersion
therefore makes these heights smaller than those of the tracer in \jeansSigmaBelowTracer\
populations, by a median factor of \jeansSigmaOverTracerMid\ at 2-6\,Gyr and
\jeansSigmaOverTracerOld\ at 8-10\,Gyr, which flattens the decline. The exception is Au19,
whose populations older than 5\,Gyr reach \auNineteenJeansOld\ of their measured heights in
these Jeans heights. Their tilt term, $(1/R)\,\partial(R\nu\sigma_{Rz}^2)/\partial R$, which we fit
with straight lines of $\ln\nu$ and $\sigma_{Rz}^2$ against $R$ over $6 < R < 10.5\kpc$, opposes the vertical force between 1 and 5\kpc, and brings them to
\auNineteenJeansTiltOld. Their mean vertical motions give thickening rates,
$\langle \mathrm{sign}(z)\,v_z\rangle/\langle|z|\rangle$, of \auNineteenThickeningOld\ per Gyr,
with errors of \auNineteenThickeningErr\ per Gyr, within the \otherThickeningOld\ per Gyr of the
populations of the same ages in the other halos. This test therefore finds no departure from a
steady state in Au19, although its errors allow the mean height to change by tens of percent
per Gyr. With the tilt term, the Jeans heights lie within 10 percent in \jeansWithinTen\
populations, with ratios of \jeansRange\ and a scatter of \jeansRms\ (Figure~\ref{fig:trends},
middle). Their trends over 2-10\,Gyr, however, are positive in \jeansPositiveTwo\ halos (hollow
diamonds), each within \jeansTrendMaxTwo\ standard errors of 0, with a mean of
\jeansTrendMeanTwo, a split of signs as improbable as that of the tracer. The tilt term matters
most in Au3, Au19 and Au27, where fitting it over $7 < R < 9.5\kpc$ or $5 < R < 11.5\kpc$, with
parabolas instead of straight lines, or setting it to 0 above the last slice moves their trends
by at most 0.02. With the tilt term, the Jeans heights therefore grow slightly faster with age
than the measured ones. Because the Jeans equation with the tilt term holds for any steady
axisymmetric population, that scatter and this trend measure departures from a steady state and
the noise of the measured profiles, which no prediction from these kinematics can remove. The
tracer's scatter of \tracerRms\ lies just above that of the Jeans heights.

\subsection{Half-mass heights}
\label{sec:halfmass}

The half-mass height needs no window, and weighs the heights above $2\hZ$ that the window leaves
out. The tracer's half-mass heights lie within 10 percent of the measured ones in
\halfMassTracerWithinTen\ populations, with a median ratio of \halfMassTracerMedian\ and a
scatter of \halfMassTracerRms, and exceed them in only \halfMassTracerAboveOne. The shortfall
is nearly the same at every age: the trends of the ratio over 0-10\,Gyr lie within two standard
errors of 0 in \halfMassTrendWithinTwo\ halos, and \halfMassTrendMaxZero\ standard errors from it in
Au19. Those over 2-10\,Gyr are the filled triangles of
Figure~\ref{fig:checks}. With half-mass heights in place of $\hZ$,
$\eta$ is \etaHalfMassRange. The populations of every age therefore carry more of their mass far
from the plane than the isothermal profile of their midplane dispersion. The Jeans heights,
which take the unclipped dispersion measured at every height, account for the excess: with the
measured $\sigz(z)$ their half-mass heights lie within 10 percent in
\halfMassJeansSigmaWithinTen\ populations, and with the tilt term in \halfMassJeansWithinTen,
with a scatter of \halfMassJeansRms. They do not separate the rise of $\sigz$ with height from
the tails of $v_z$ that the clipping removes at the plane (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). The sheet comes
within 10 percent in \halfMassSheetWithinTen, with a median of \halfMassSheetMedian, so its
offsets do not come from the window.

\subsection{Disk stars, finer bins and clipping}
\label{sec:robustness}

Three checks ask whether the residual over 2-10\,Gyr comes from how the populations are
selected and measured. Each repeats the measurement in its own bins with 100 bootstrap
resamplings. Figure~\ref{fig:checks} sets the trends of each check next to those of the
tracer, so that a residual that came from the selection or the measurement would move toward 0.
Keeping only the stars whose circularity $L_z/L_c(E)$ exceeds 0.7 keeps
\diskMassRange\ of the mass of a bin, the least in the oldest bins. With these stars alone, the
tracer predicts $\hZ$ within 10 percent in \diskWithinTen\ bins. Its trends over 0-10\,Gyr lie
within \diskTrendMaxZero\ standard errors of 0, whereas over 2-10\,Gyr \diskTrendNegativeTwo\ are
negative (hollow circles). In bins of 0.25\,Gyr, \fineTrendNegativeTwo\ of the trends over
2-10\,Gyr are negative, up to \fineTrendMaxTwo\ standard errors from 0 (filled squares). Without
clipping, $\sigz$ keeps the tails of $v_z$, which raise the tracer heights by \unclippedShift,
so that some populations of 1\,Gyr have heavy tails in $v_z$.
The tracer of the unclipped dispersion comes within 10 percent in \unclippedWithinTen\ bins,
with trends over 2-10\,Gyr that are negative in \unclippedTrendNegativeTwo\ halos (hollow
squares). The residual is therefore a property of the
disk stars, persists in populations of 0.25\,Gyr, and does not come from the clipping, which
leaves the rise of $\sigz$ with height as its cause.

\subsection{The shape of the vertical actions}
\label{sec:shape}

\citet{TingRix2026} describe a coeval population by the distribution of its vertical actions,
$p(J_z) \propto \exp[-(J_z/J_0)^{2-b}]$, which diffusion with a coefficient $\propto J_z^b$ leaves
behind. Scatterers that fill the volume, such as bending waves or dark substructure, give $b = 1$,
whereas scatterers confined to a thin layer at the midplane, such as giant molecular clouds, give
$b = 1/2$, close to the $b = 0.51$ they measure for red-clump stars of 2-8\,Gyr in the Milky Way.
\citet{DjuricSchoenrich2026} likewise derive vertical distribution functions in the actions of
coeval populations scattered by giant molecular clouds, which depart from the isothermal shape. We fit $b$ and $J_0$ to the vertical actions of
the stars of each bin by maximum likelihood, weighted by initial mass, with the actions from the
St\"ackel fudge of AGAMA in the fitted potential \citep{Binney2012}. An isothermal population is
exponential in $J_z$ only where the potential is harmonic: in a potential $\propto |z|^p$ its
energy grows as $J_z^{2p/(p+2)}$, so a linear potential gives it $b = 4/3$. We therefore read each
fitted $b$ against $b_\mathrm{iso}$, the $b$ of the isothermal tracer of the bin's $\sigz$ in the
same potential, fitted to its distribution of actions in the same way. $b_\mathrm{iso}$, and the
heights of the population with $b = 1/2$ below, need the orbits of the population to stay inside
the 12\kpc\ of the grid of heights on which we compute every profile, which those of \coevalCount\ populations do.

In these populations the fitted $b$ is \bFitWithIsoRange\ and exceeds $b_\mathrm{iso}$ in
\bAboveIso, by a median of \bExcessMedian. The cold populations, with $\hZ < 1\kpc$, have $b$ of \bColdRange, against $b_\mathrm{iso}$ of
\bIsoColdRange. The vertical actions of the Auriga populations thus have heavier tails than an
isothermal population of the same midplane dispersion, as their half-mass heights showed, and
depart from it in the direction opposite to the thin layer. The population with $b = 1/2$ makes
the same point through the heights. With $J_0$ set so that its stars within 0.3\kpc\ of the plane
have the $\sigz$ of the bin, its flat-topped velocities make it thicker than the tracer near the
plane. It predicts $\hZ$ within 10 percent in \coevalWithinTen\ populations, with a median
ratio of \coevalMedian, whereas over the same populations the tracer does so in
\coevalTracerWithinTen, with a median of \coevalTracerMedian. The Auriga populations are
therefore not thin-layer populations, which is not surprising in hindsight, because the
star-forming gas of these simulations is a two-phase medium described by a subgrid model rather
than resolved giant molecular clouds \citep{Grand2017}.

\subsection{The sheet}
\label{sec:sheet}

Against the measured heights, the sheet comes within 10 percent in \sheetWithinTen\
populations, with ratios of \sheetRange: \sheetYoung\ for the youngest population of each halo
and \sheetOld\ for the oldest. In the bottom panel of Figure~\ref{fig:trends} its ratios rise
with age through the band of agreement. Its trends with age over 0-10\,Gyr are
\sheetTrendRange, at least \sheetTrendMin\ standard errors from 0 in every halo, as expected of a
ratio that grows as $\sigz^{2-\eta}$, or $\mathrm{age}^{(2-\eta)\beta_z}$ at fixed $\Sigma$. The
sheet thus matches a population at intermediate ages, where $\sigz^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$ crosses its
height, and predicts the young populations thinner and the old ones thicker than measured. This
is not surprising in hindsight, because the sheet is the tracer of a layer whose own mass makes
its potential, whereas a population of one age is one part of that layer and moves in the
potential of all of it.

\section{Whole disks}
\label{sec:whole}

The relation of \citet{Palicio2025} uses the height of all the stars of a disk, which mixes
populations of every age. If every population is an isothermal tracer, the profile of the whole
disk is their sum, whose height follows from the kinematics of its populations and the
potential alone. Models of the Milky Way disk are built this way, from isothermal components of
different ages with dispersions prescribed by an age-velocity relation
\citep{Robin2003,JustJahreiss2010,SysoliatinaJust2022}. Here the dispersions are measured rather
than prescribed, so that the sum can be tested against heights measured independently. We split the radial range of
\citet[Sect.~3.4]{Palicio2025} of each halo, $0.2L \le R < L$ with $L$ from their Table~1, into rings of 1\kpc, and each ring into bins of
1\,Gyr from 0 to 14\,Gyr. Each cell enters as the tracer of its clipped $\sigz$ within 0.3\kpc\
of the plane, in the fitted potential at the center of its ring, with the current mass of its
stars, as we read the histograms of stellar mass of \citet{Palicio2025}. A cell with fewer than 30 stars
within 0.3\kpc\ of the plane has no $\sigz$ and is left out, which removes at most
\sumUnmodeledMax\ of the mass of a disk. Both the sum and the stars are fitted in the window of
Section~\ref{sec:estimator}, with errors from 50 bootstrap draws of the stars.

One tracer per ring, with the dispersion of all its stars, predicts \sumSingleScaled\ of the
measured height of the whole disk, above it in \sumSingleAboveOne\ disks. The sum over rings and ages predicts \sumMixtureScaled, with a median of
\sumMixtureMedian. Figure~\ref{fig:flare} repeats the comparison in radial ranges 2 to 4\kpc\
wide. In the top panel, the height of the sum (dotted lines) follows the measured height of each
disk (solid lines) as both rise with radius. The bottom panel shows their ratio, within 10
percent in \sumRingsWithinTen\ radial ranges.
The heights of whole disks over the radial ranges of \citet{Palicio2025}, measured in the
self-consistent window rather than in their fixed windows, thus follow from the tracers of their
populations.

\section{The Milky Way}
\label{sec:milkyway}

\subsection{Star counts split by age}
\label{sec:counts}

A spectroscopic survey cannot give the density of a population against $|z|$ without its
selection function, whereas a photometric sample can if it is complete, as we take the Gaia
giants below to be. We count the Gaia DR3
\citep{Gaia2016,Gaia2023} giants of a cylinder of radius 1.5\kpc\ around the Sun, within
$|z| < 1.2\kpc$, whose reddening-free Wesenheit absolute magnitude
$M_W = G - 1.9\,(G_\mathrm{BP} - G_\mathrm{RP}) + 5\log_{10}(\varpi/\mathrm{mas}) - 10$ lies in a
window. They have parallaxes above 0.5\,mas measured to better than 20 percent, $G < 16$ and
$G_\mathrm{BP} - G_\mathrm{RP} > 0.9$. We take their distances as $1/\varpi$. We split each of
12 slices of $|z|$, 0.1\kpc\ thick, into populations with the fraction of its spectroscopic stars
that belong to each, interpolated between the centers of the slices. The primary red-clump stars of LAMOST \citep{Huang2020} with ages better than 50 percent split
the
\lamostGaia\ Gaia stars within 0.3\,mag of the red-clump peak of $M_W$, which we fit in each slice of $|z|$, through \lamostMatched\
matches by position and $G$. APOGEE DR17 \citep{Majewski2017,Abdurrouf2022} splits the
\apogeeGaia\ giants of the box $-2.6 < M_W < -0.9$ through matches by Gaia source identifier,
with the ages of astroNN \citep{Mackereth2019} (\apogeeMatched\ matches) and those of DistMass
\citep{StoneMartinez2024}, which \citet{Imig2025} adopt (\distmassMatched\ matches). APOGEE
selects its targets by magnitude and color rather than by age \citep{Zasowski2013,Zasowski2017}.
As long as the targeting of a survey does not depend on age at a fixed position, the fraction of a
population in a slice is unbiased even though the completeness of the survey changes with $|z|$,
an assumption that Section~\ref{sec:mwchecks} tests for both surveys. A
population is a range of 2\,Gyr of ages of the low-$[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}]$ disk, below
$[\mathrm{Mg/Fe}] = 0.15 - 0.10\,[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$ with APOGEE and
$[\alpha/\mathrm{Fe}] = 0.12 - 0.05\,[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$ with LAMOST, and with $[\mathrm{Fe/H}] > -0.6$. Its fraction varies across the cylinder as $e^{gx}$, with $x$ the
distance toward the Galactic center and one gradient $g$ per population fitted to its matches.

We fit $\hZ$ to the counts in the window of Section~\ref{sec:estimator}. The kinematics come
from the giants of each population in the solar annulus $7.5 < R < 9\kpc$ with distances better
than 10 percent and, with APOGEE, ages better than 40 percent, with the distances and velocities of astroNN for APOGEE and of \citet{Huang2020},
from LAMOST radial velocities and Gaia proper motions, for LAMOST: $\sigz$ from those with
$|z| < 0.3\kpc$, clipped and with their median velocity error removed in quadrature, with every
star in the Galactocentric frame of astroNN, the Sun at $R_0 = \rZero\kpc$ and 20.8\,pc above the
plane \citep{BennettBovy2019}. The tracer of each population, in the potential of
\citet{McMillan2017} at $R_0$, goes through the same counts, slice by slice, as do the sheet and
the Jeans equation, so that a predicted and a measured height are fitted the same way. Errors
come from \nBootstrap\ bootstrap draws of the Gaia stars, their matches and the kinematic stars.
In the oldest bin on the DistMass scale, \distmassFailedEightOneZero\ of the draws give no
height, because that bin lies close to the 0.6\kpc\ that the counts can measure.

\subsection{The tracer and the Jeans equation}
\label{sec:mwtracer}

Figure~\ref{fig:milkyway} sets the predictions against the counts as Figure~\ref{fig:trends} does
for Auriga, one panel per survey and age scale. In the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} (hollow
circles), the tracer predicts the counts with ratios of \lamostTracer\ with LAMOST, \apogeeTracer\
with APOGEE on the astroNN scale and \distmassTracer\ on the DistMass scale, with trends over
0-10\,Gyr of \lamostTracerTrendZero, \apogeeTracerTrendZero\ and \distmassTracerTrendZero, and
over 2-10\,Gyr of \lamostTracerTrendTwo, \apogeeTracerTrendTwo\ and \distmassTracerTrendTwo, and
$\eta$ of \lamostEta, \apogeeEta\ and \distmassEta, against \mwTracerEta\ for the heights of the
tracer over the same bins. It
falls below the counts, however, in \tracerBelowOne\ bins, with medians of \tracerMedians\
against \tracerMedian\ in Auriga. Two causes could make it fall
short: populations that are not isothermal, and a potential denser near the plane than measured.
The Jeans equation with $\sigz^2$ measured in the slices $0$-$0.3$, $0.3$-$0.6$, $0.6$-$1.0$ and
$1.0$-$1.5\kpc$, without the tilt term, which this sample cannot measure, gives heights of
\jeansRatios\ of the counts, with medians of \jeansMedians\ (filled squares), \jeansOverTracer\ times
the heights of the tracer in \jeansBelowTracer\ bins. The dispersions of our kinematic stars rise
with height in every bin, from 10-25\kms\ in the slice nearest the plane to 19-28\kms\ in the
farthest. As in Auriga (Section~\ref{sec:jeans}), the prefactor $\sigz^2(0)/\sigz^2(z)$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:jeansdensity}) lowers the density wherever the dispersion has risen, by more than the slower fall
of its exponential raises it over the heights of these populations. The change of $\sigz$ with
height thus deepens the shortfall rather than removing it. The sheet, with the
\sigmaSurf\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ of \citet{McMillan2017} within 1.1\kpc, predicts
\mwSheetRange\ of the counts, from the youngest to the oldest bins, with trends over 0-10\,Gyr of
\mwSheetTrendsZero.

\subsection{A family of potentials}
\label{sec:family}

The potential accounts for the shortfall. At $R_0$, the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} has a
midplane density of \mcmillanRhoZero\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ and \mcmillanSigmaEleven\,$\Msun\,
\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ within $|z| < 1.1\kpc$, above the $\rho_0 = 0.097 \pm 0.013\,\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$
of \citet[their Eq.~57]{McKee2015} and the $\Sigma_{1.1} = 68 \pm 4\,\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ of
\citet{BovyRix2013}. Where the potential is harmonic the height of a tracer scales as
$\rho_0^{-1/2}$, so a midplane density 20 percent above 0.097 thins a cold population by about
9 percent. We vary the surface density of the thin disk of \citet{McMillan2017} by factors of
0.7, 0.8, 0.9 and 1.0, its scale height over 0.25, 0.30 and 0.35\kpc, and the normalization of its dark
halo by factors of 0.7, 1.0 and 1.3. Of these potentials, \familyAdmissible\ lie within one
error of both measurements, with $\rho_0$ of \familyRhoZero\ and $\Sigma_{1.1}$ of
\familySigmaEleven. In them the tracer rises to the dark gray band of Figure~\ref{fig:milkyway},
with medians over the counts of \familyTracerMedians. In the oldest APOGEE bin on the astroNN and
the DistMass scale, \familyOldestMissing\ of these potentials make the tracer thicker than the
0.6\kpc\ that the counts below 1.2\kpc\ can measure, so their medians leave that bin out. On the
astroNN scale the other potentials predict \familyOldestAstroNN\ times the measured height of
that bin, \familyOldestAstroNNSigmas\ standard errors above it if each carries the error of the
tracer in the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} there. The shortfall of the tracer can therefore come from the densities of the potential near the Sun
rather than from the populations, although populations with $b = 1/2$ would match the counts in
the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} as well (Section~\ref{sec:mwshape}). The family is not refitted to the
rotation curve: its admissible members, whose disks or halos are lighter than those of
\citet{McMillan2017}, have circular velocities at $R_0$ of \familyVCirc\kms, below its
\mcmillanVCirc\kms. The tracer needs only the vertical potential near
the plane, which $\rho_0$ and $\Sigma_{1.1}$ bound.

\subsection{The local potential from the tracer}
\label{sec:inversion}

The family also turns the test around. Every bin carries the ratio of the tracer to its $\hZ$ in
each of the 36 potentials. Across the family, the logarithm of that ratio follows a linear
function of $\ln\rho_0$ and $\ln\Sigma_{1.1}$, with a root-mean-square residual over the 36
potentials of at most \inversionModelRms\ in every bin that enters the fit below. If the populations
are isothermal tracers, one potential brings every bin of a survey to its measured $\hZ$, which a
weighted least-squares fit of the bins finds. A bin enters the fit when the tracer has a height
in all 36 potentials. The oldest APOGEE bin on either age scale does not, because some of the
lighter potentials make its tracer thicker than the counts can measure, so the fit takes
\inversionBins\ bins of the three surveys. This is the inverse use of the tracer that
\citet{BovyRix2013} made with mono-abundance populations, with the family standing in for a
parameterized potential. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} shows the midplane density that this fit
returns for each survey. With the isothermal tracer (filled circles) it is
\inversionRhoZero\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ with LAMOST and with APOGEE on the astroNN and the
DistMass scale, against the $0.097 \pm 0.013\,\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ (gray band) that
\citet{McKee2015} find by combining
their census of stars, remnants and gas with a density of dark matter inferred from dynamical
measurements of the surface density. $\Sigma_{1.1}$ is not constrained, at
\inversionSigmaEleven\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$, and correlates with $\rho_0$ at
\inversionCorrelation\ in each fit: below about 1\kpc, where the populations stay, a denser
midplane with less mass above it gives nearly the potential of a lighter midplane with more mass,
a freedom that the errors of $\rho_0$ include. With LAMOST the fitted $\Sigma_{1.1}$ lies outside
the \familySigmaElevenAll\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$ of the family, so that its $\rho_0$
extrapolates the linear dependence of the ratios on the two densities. The $\chi^2$ per degree of
freedom is \inversionChiTwo, so with LAMOST and on the DistMass scale the bins do not all agree
with one potential within their errors, as the zigzag of the LAMOST ratios with age in
Figure~\ref{fig:milkyway} also shows. The quoted errors, scaled by the square root of the
$\chi^2$ per degree of freedom, absorb this scatter without explaining it.

\subsection{The shape of the Milky Way populations}
\label{sec:mwshape}

In the potential of \citet{McMillan2017}, at the dispersions of our bins, the isothermal tracer
has $b_\mathrm{iso}$ of \bIsoMw, so the $b = 0.51$ of \citet{TingRix2026} departs from it by about
0.6. The population with $b = 1/2$ and the $\sigz$ of each bin, through the same counts, gives
medians of \coevalMedians\ in the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} and \familyCoevalMedians\ in
the admissible potentials, against \tracerMedians\ and \familyTracerMedians\ for the isothermal
tracer. Being thicker than the tracer, it exceeds the 0.6\kpc\ that the counts can measure in
more bins: in the oldest bin of each survey it does so in \coevalOldestMissing\ of the admissible
potentials with LAMOST and with APOGEE on the astroNN and the DistMass scale, and on the astroNN
scale in the potential of \citet{McMillan2017} as well, so these medians leave those bins out. Inverted as above, from \inversionCoevalBins\ bins, it needs midplane densities of
\inversionCoevalRhoZero\,$\Msun\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ (hollow circles in Figure~\ref{fig:inversion}).
Being thicker than the isothermal population of the same midplane dispersion,
it fits the counts best in potentials denser than the measurements near the Sun, close to that of
\citet{McMillan2017}, by \inversionCoevalSigmas\ combined standard errors. At the measured densities the counts favor the isothermal shape, although
the error of $\rho_0$ keeps the preference weak, so the star counts of the solar neighborhood do
not by themselves decide the shape that \citet{TingRix2026} measure in the actions.


\subsection{Age errors and selection}
\label{sec:mwchecks}

The LAMOST ages, which we keep up to a relative error of 50 percent, carry typical errors of 30
percent \citep{Huang2020}. The APOGEE ages are cut at a relative error of 40 percent for the
kinematic stars only, so the matches that split the counts keep every age. Bins of 2\,Gyr
therefore mix their neighbors. The targeting of either
survey could also depend on age at a fixed position. Two checks remeasure every survey, with
\checkBootstrap\ bootstrap draws per bin. The first draws every age again from its error,
lognormal with the relative error as the dispersion of $\ln$ age, which doubles the variance of
the errors, in \ageRealizations\ realizations. The medians of the tracer over the counts become
\ageTracerMedians, against \tracerMedians\ with the ages as given. Its trends over 0-10\,Gyr become
\ageTracerTrendsZero, within \ageTracerTrendMaxZero\ standard errors of 0. Doubling the variance
of the age errors thus leaves the test where it was, presumably because the mixing of ages enters the
counts and the kinematics of a bin alike.

The second asks whether the fraction of a population at a fixed position depends on the
magnitude of its stars, as it could if the targeting depended on age there. In each slice of
$|z|$ we split the matches of each survey at their median $G$, and measure each survey with each
half. The heights of the bright over the faint half are \magnitudeRatioRange, within two
standard errors in \magnitudeWithinTwo\ bins and at most \magnitudeMaxSigmas\ standard errors
apart: \magnitudeLamostWithinTwo\ with LAMOST, and \magnitudeApogeeWithinTwo\ with APOGEE on either
age scale. A targeting of APOGEE that depends on age at a fixed position may thus move some of
its bins by up to the largest of these differences, whereas the LAMOST fractions show no
dependence on magnitude. The test detects a targeting by age only where it also changes with
magnitude.

\section{Comparison with Imig et al.}
\label{sec:imig}

\citet{Imig2025} fit every population of APOGEE DR17 with more than 100 stars, in bins of
$[\alpha/\mathrm{M}]$, $[\mathrm{M/H}]$ and DistMass age, with a model of the whole footprint of the
survey corrected for its selection function. Their model of a population is
$\nu(R, z) = \nu_\odot \Sigma(R)\exp[-|z|/h_Z(R)]$, with a broken exponential $\Sigma(R)$ and a
scale height that flares linearly. Their heights at the Sun are therefore the scales of
exponentials fitted to the whole range of $|z|$ that APOGEE reaches, whereas ours are windowed
heights of counts below $|z| = 1.2\kpc$. An isothermal population is flat at the plane and falls
more steeply above, so the exponential that matches its mean height is thinner than its windowed
height: for the tracer of \citet{McMillan2017} at $R_0$, the windowed height is \estimatorRatios\
times the exponential fit below 1.2\kpc\ for $\sigz$ of \estimatorSigmas\kms. We therefore compare each of their populations with our
counts of the same cell of age and $[\mathrm{M/H}]$, both fitted with the same exponential below
$|z| = 1.2\kpc$, their model put through our cylinder and counts. We take their populations below
the $[\alpha/\mathrm{M}]$ of their Eq.~(1), give each cell the $\sigz$ of its giants within
0.3\kpc\ of the plane, and measure a growth at fixed metallicity as the slope of $\ln\hZ$
against $\ln\sigz$ with one intercept per bin of $[\mathrm{M/H}]$. Of their cells, \cellsMeasured\
have at least \cellsMin\ matched stars and a height in both our counts and their model.

Fitted the same way, our counts over their model have a median of \cellsExpMedian, and change by
\cellsExpTrend\ per dex of metallicity at fixed age, so the two measurements agree in level and
in how the heights depend on metallicity. The two differ in how the heights grow. At fixed
metallicity our exponential fits over theirs change by \cellsExpTrendAge\ per $\ln$ age, from
a median of \cellsExpYoung\ below 2.51\,Gyr to one of \cellsExpOld\ above 5.01\,Gyr. Figure~\ref{fig:imig} shows
how the heights grow with the $\sigz$ of the giants of each cell. Our exponential heights
(filled circles) grow as \dispersionCounts\ at fixed metallicity, whereas their published
heights (hollow circles) grow as \dispersionSun, consistent with $\hZ \propto \sigz$ (dotted
line), the growth of an isothermal population in a harmonic potential. The exponential fit to
the tracer, however, grows with exponents of \familyGrowthWindow\ between 10 and 25\kms\ across
the admissible potentials of Section~\ref{sec:family} (dashed lines), and of \familyGrowthWhole\ over the whole profile, because the hotter
populations reach heights where the potential is no longer harmonic, and the cut at 1.2\kpc\
steepens the growth further. Our exponential heights
also grow more slowly than this fit to the tracer, by \countsBelowTracerGrowth\ standard errors,
whereas the windowed heights of our age bins grow as the tracer does
(Section~\ref{sec:mwtracer}). We have not traced the difference between the two estimates. A potential that is denser
or lighter near the plane within the measured errors moves the heights of all the populations by
similar factors, which changes their level more than their growth, so the uncertainty of the
potential does not account for the difference. The targeting of APOGEE does not account for the growth of our
heights either, because split by LAMOST, whose targeting and ages differ, our exponential fits
over theirs change by \lamostExpTrendAge\ per $\ln$ age. This tests our side of the comparison
only, because their heights rest on the selection function of APOGEE.

Part of the difference can come from the range of the fits. Their fit takes the stars at every
height that APOGEE reaches, whereas ours stops at 1.2\kpc. For the tracer, the exponential
fit to the whole profile is \estimatorWhole\ times the fit below 1.2\kpc, the lowest for the
hottest population, so their old heights should come out thinner than ours by up to that
factor. That would account for part of the excess of our old cells, but not for the young cells,
where our fits are the thinner. We have not traced the rest. Both measurements use the same survey
on the same age scale, so the errors of the ages mix the same neighbors into both, whereas the two
measurements differ in where the stars lie: their height at the Sun comes from a model of the
whole footprint, in which each population has one exponential in $|z|$ at every radius and a
linear flare, whereas ours comes from the stars within 1.5\kpc\ of the Sun. A fit of their model
to the stars near the Sun alone would show whether the difference comes from the rest of the
footprint.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Age-thickness relations}

The heights of mono-age populations grow with age as $\mathrm{age}^{\eta\beta_z}$, rather than as
the $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$ of the sheet. Over 0-10\,Gyr in the Auriga halos, $\sigz$ grows with
exponents $\beta_z$ of \betaZRangeZero\ and $\hZ$ with exponents $\beta_h$ of \betaHRangeZero. The
products $\eta\beta_z$ are \etaBetaZRangeZero, whereas $2\beta_z$ would be \twoBetaZRangeZero. In
the Milky Way our counts give $\beta_z$ of \mwBetaZ\ and $\beta_h$ of \mwBetaH, against $\eta\beta_z$
of \mwEtaBetaZ\ and $2\beta_z$ of \mwTwoBetaZ. Because $\eta$ and $\beta_h$ come from the same
heights, they would agree exactly if the heights and the dispersions followed power laws in age,
so their agreement is a consistency of the fits rather than a test. $\beta_h - \eta\beta_z$ is
\betaHMinusEtaBetaZ, largest in Au19 and Au27, whose oldest populations thicken abruptly. The test is that $\eta$
follows the local slope of the tracer of each potential (Section~\ref{sec:eta}). The exponent of
the sheet exceeds that of the tracer by a factor of $2/\eta$, about 1.6-1.8 here.
\citet[Sect.~5.3]{Martig2026} note that the sheet would make the half-mass heights of the stars of
lenticular galaxies grow with age with an exponent between 0.8 and 1, unlike the heights they
measure. A tracer lowers that exponent by the factor $2/\eta$, although no value of $\eta$ produces
the flat half-mass heights of their younger populations, so the tracer does not by itself account
for their measurements.

\subsection{The shape of coeval populations}

Two departures from isothermality bear on this work, in opposite directions. In Auriga, the
dispersion of every population grows with height (Section~\ref{sec:jeans}), the populations carry
more mass far from the plane than the isothermal profile of their midplane dispersion
(Section~\ref{sec:halfmass}), and their vertical actions have heavier tails than those of an
isothermal population (Section~\ref{sec:shape}). In the Milky Way, \citet{TingRix2026} find actions with a
sharper cutoff, $b = 0.51$, which they attribute to heating by molecular clouds.
\citet{Sharma2021} find that the dispersions of stars of fixed age and metallicity rise with
height, whereas \citet{Bovy2012} find mono-abundance populations nearly isothermal in $|z|$. The
dispersions of our Milky Way kinematic stars rise with height in every bin
(Section~\ref{sec:mwtracer}). Our star counts alone do not separate these shapes: the population with $b = 1/2$ fits them best in a potential
denser at the midplane than measured, close to that of \citet{McMillan2017}, whereas the
isothermal tracer fits them at the measured density. For the heights, the shape is a correction of order 10 percent at
fixed $\sigz$, small against the ratios of \sheetRange\ between the sheet and the measured heights, whereas for the
dynamics of disk heating the shape is the record that \citet{TingRix2026} read.

\subsection{The radial action and the scale height}

For a population of one age, the slope $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/\hZ$ of the relation between radial
action and scale height grows with age as $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_R - \eta\beta_z}$, with $\sigR \propto \mathrm{age}^{\beta_R}$, for a tracer in a
fixed potential, rather than the $\mathrm{age}^{2(\beta_R - \beta_z)}$ of the sheet at fixed
$\Sigma$, so the derivation of the slope through the sheet, made for whole disks, does not carry
over to single populations. Across galaxies, the heights of whole disks follow from the tracers of
their populations (Section~\ref{sec:whole}). With a proxy for the reference radial action $J_{R,w}$ of
\citet{Palicio2025}, the median of the cells of a face-on map of the median $\JR$ over
$0.2L \le R < L$, with $L$ the radial extent of each galaxy in their Table~1, the
slope against the self-consistent
heights of the seven halos is \slopeScaled. Their fit across 23 disks gives $3.69 \times 10^{-2}$
in units of the angular momentum of the Sun per kpc, or \palicioSlope\kms\ with the
$8.249\kpc \times 238.5\kms$ that \citet{Palicio2023} take for it. It depends, however,
on one halo, \influentialHalo, without which it is \withoutInfluentialScaled, so seven halos do not
test the relation across galaxies.

\subsection{Limitations}

The Auriga snapshots are at the standard resolution of the public release, whose gravitational
softening of star and dark matter particles, 369\,pc since $z = 1$ \citep{Grand2017}, is close to
the heights of \youngHeights\kpc\ of the populations younger than 1\Gyr. The test needs the
potential that the stars feel, not a Newtonian one. The potential of the snapshot, which the
simulation computes with the softening, changes the tracer of the \nearSofteningCount\ populations
thinner than twice the softening by \nearSofteningSnapshot. Whether the heights are those that the
simulated galaxies would have without softening is a question of convergence, which
\citet[Sect.~6]{Grand2017} address for three halos: the rms heights of Au6 change negligibly with
a softening ten times smaller, whereas those of Au16, one of our halos, show tentative signs of
thinning at higher resolution. \citet{Pakmor2025} find that the
rise of the velocity dispersion at large radii seen at that resolution might be entirely numerical.
Numerical heating raises the dispersion and the height of a population together, so it shifts both
sides of the test, but it could change $\eta$ and the shape of the actions, which the Level 3 runs
of the release, at higher resolution, would test. Every Auriga population comes from one
annulus per halo, so we do not know how $\eta$ depends on radius. The fitted potential is
axisymmetric, which the potential of the snapshot averaged over azimuth checks but cannot make
exact for the bar of Au23. In the Milky Way, the family of potentials varies the thin disk and the
halo of \citet{McMillan2017} but neither its thick disk nor its gas, and is not refitted to the
rotation curve. The surface density of \citet{BovyRix2013} comes from stars modeled in
equilibrium, so the agreement of the tracer with the counts remains one of consistency with that
assumption. The inversion constrains $\rho_0$ but not $\Sigma_{1.1}$. The Jeans equation of the
Milky Way leaves out the tilt term, which held up the old populations of Au19. The fractions
of LAMOST come from primary red-clump stars, whereas the Gaia stars within 0.3\,mag of the
red-clump peak also include giants of other stages, whose mix of ages can differ and change
with $|z|$. Finally, we have not
found why our heights and those of \citet{Imig2025} grow differently with age.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item In seven Auriga disks, the isothermal tracer in a potential fitted to all the particles
predicts the scale heights of \tracerWithinTen\ of \populations\ populations of 1\,Gyr within 10
percent, with a scatter of \tracerRms, just above the \jeansRms\ of the vertical Jeans equation
with the measured dispersion profile and tilt term. The self-gravitating sheet does
so for \sheetWithinTen, predicting young populations thinner and old ones thicker than measured.
\item The heights grow as $\sigz^\eta$ with $\eta$ of \etaRange, which equals within two standard
errors the local slope of the tracer of the potential in \etaChordWithinTwo\ halos, so
age-thickness relations grow as $\mathrm{age}^{\eta\beta_z}$ rather than $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$.
\item The populations are not exactly isothermal. Their dispersions rise with height, they carry
more mass far from the plane than the isothermal profile of their midplane dispersion, and their
vertical actions have heavier tails. The ratio of the tracer to their heights declines slightly
with age over 2-10\,Gyr, with the disk stars alone and in bins of 0.25\,Gyr as well, which the
Jeans equation with the measured $\sigz(z)$ removes, except in Au19, where it needs the tilt
term. With the tilt term, the Jeans heights grow slightly faster with age than the measured ones.
\item Summed over ages, the tracers predict \sumMixtureScaled\ of the heights of whole disks and
follow their flare.
\item In the solar neighborhood, Gaia DR3 star counts split by LAMOST and APOGEE ages follow the
tracer with no trend with age in the potential of \citet{McMillan2017}, at medians of
\familyTracerMedians\ in potentials that match the measured local densities, and imply midplane densities of \inversionRhoZero\,$\Msun\,
\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$. Doubling the variance of the age errors leaves the ratios of the tracer to the counts in
the potential of \citet{McMillan2017}, and their trends with age, in place. Splitting the
age tracers at their median magnitude moves no LAMOST height by more than two standard errors,
and \magnitudeApogeeWithinTwo\ APOGEE heights by less than two, the others by up to
\magnitudeMaxSigmas.
\item Fitted the same way, our counts and the model of \citet{Imig2025} agree in level and in
the dependence of the heights on metallicity, but differ in how the heights grow with age, a
difference that neither the potential nor the targeting of APOGEE behind our counts accounts for
and that the range of the fits accounts for only in part.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The window of the fit}
\label{app:window}

The scale height of a profile that is not exponential depends on the window in which it is
fitted. A $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ layer is flat for $|z| \ll z_0$ and falls as
$\exp(-2|z|/z_0)$ above, so a window much wider than $z_0$ returns a scale close to the mean
height of the whole profile, $z_0 \ln 2$, whereas a window narrower than $z_0$ holds an almost
flat profile, which the fit reads as a very large scale, or as no scale at all when the mean
height reaches $L/2$. We drew \injectionDraws\ samples of \injectionStars\ heights from an
exponential, a $\mathrm{sech}^2$ layer, and a mixture of two exponentials, and fitted each in a
fixed window, $L = Z_\mathrm{lim} = 1\kpc$, and in the self-consistent window $L = 2\hZ$.
Figure~\ref{fig:window} shows the ratio of the two heights against the thickness of the profile
relative to the window (top), and the self-consistent height against that of the exact,
noise-free profile (bottom). The fixed window recovers exponentials within
\exponentialFixedBias\ of their scale, as the maximum-likelihood estimate of an exponential
should, whereas it returns \sechTwoThin\ times the self-consistent height of a $\mathrm{sech}^2$
layer that is thin against the window and \sechTwoWindow\ times that of one as thick as the
window. For profiles thicker than the window, the fixed window finds no height in up to
\fixedFailedMax\ of the draws, which the medians of Figure~\ref{fig:window} leave out. The self-consistent window recovers every injected profile within
\selfConsistentBias. The Auriga populations (dots in the top panel), each fitted in
the fixed window of its halo, follow the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ curve: below
$\hZ = 0.5\,Z_\mathrm{lim}$ their median ratio of the fixed to the self-consistent height is
\windowLowHalos, against \windowLowSechTwo\ for $\mathrm{sech}^2$ and \windowLowExponential\ for an
exponential, as expected of tracers in a potential that is harmonic near the plane. The
windows of \citet[Table~1]{Palicio2025}, with $Z_\mathrm{lim}$ of \zLimRange\kpc\ chosen for each
galaxy, are fixed in this sense.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{paper-window.pdf}
\caption{A window of fixed size biases the scale height of any profile that is not exponential, by an amount that depends on how thick the profile is against the window, whereas the self-consistent window $L = 2\hZ$ recovers every injected profile within \selfConsistentBias. Top: the logarithm of the height fitted in a fixed window $Z_\mathrm{lim} = 1\kpc$ over the self-consistent height, against $\hZ/Z_\mathrm{lim}$, for exponentials (solid), $\mathrm{sech}^2$ layers (dashed) and mixtures of two exponentials (dotted), each the median of \injectionDraws\ draws of \injectionStars\ heights with a dark gray band between the 16th and 84th percentiles, and for the Auriga populations (dots), each fitted in the fixed window of its halo, with $Z_\mathrm{lim}$ of \zLimRange\kpc, which follow the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ curve where they are thin against the window. Bottom: the logarithm of the self-consistent height of the injected draws over that of the exact, noise-free profile. The light gray bands mark agreement within 10 percent.}
\label{fig:window}
\end{figure}

The self-consistent window takes the smallest $L$ that satisfies $L = 2h(L)$. With few stars
inside $L/2$, the profile of a hot population is nearly flat inside a small window, so that the
noise of the fitted $1/h$ can satisfy the condition by chance, far below the true scale. In
3000 draws of 300 stars from an exponential of $h = 3\kpc$, a minimum of 20 stars inside $L/2$
gave a root below 1\kpc\ in 25 draws, a minimum of 50 in 1 and a minimum of 100 in none,
which is the minimum we use.

\section{The mock galaxy and the potential}
\label{app:mock}

We tested the analysis on a mock snapshot of a known axisymmetric galaxy, written in the units
and the file layout of Auriga, tilted, moving, and close to two faces of the periodic box. The
analysis recovers the spin axis within \mockSpin\ degrees, the circular velocity of the annulus
within \mockVc, $\dPhi$ at the median radius within \mockPhi\ over $0.05 < z \le 1.5\kpc$, and
the height and the median radial action of every bin within \mockHz\ and \mockJr\ of the truth.
In the real snapshots, the potential averaged over eight sectors of azimuth differs from the
axisymmetric fit by \residualRange\,$(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2$ between the plane and $z = 1\kpc$,
and by at most \residualMax\,$(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2$ up to \residualTop\kpc, the last slice we
measure, above which we hold the difference fixed. The tracer in the potential of the snapshot is
therefore \snapshotRange\ times the tracer in the fit, even with the bar of Au23.
