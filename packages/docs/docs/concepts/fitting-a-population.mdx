# Fitting a population

Every number of the Auriga part of the paper starts from the star particles of one population:
its dispersion near the plane, its scale height, and the heights that the three predictions give
it. This page goes through those steps for one population at a time (`experiments.populations`),
with the particles, the fit and the predictions drawn together for any population of any of the
seven halos.

## The steps

1. The annulus. The star particles with $7.5 < R < 9$ kpc and $|z| < 12$ kpc, in the frame of the
   disk, form the sample of a halo. Their median radius within 2.5 kpc of the plane is
   $R_\mathrm{mid}$, where the potential is evaluated.
2. The population. The particles are split into ten bins of 1 Gyr of age, from 0 to 10 Gyr, by the
   time since their formation. Ages in the simulation are exact, so a bin holds the stars born in
   one Gyr, whatever has happened to them since.
3. The dispersion. $\sigma_z$ is the dispersion of $v_z$ of the particles of the bin with
   $|z| < 0.3$ kpc, after clipping those more than 4 standard deviations from the median, with
   the standard deviation estimated from the median absolute deviation (`clipped_dispersion`).
   Keeping the tails of $v_z$ that the clipping removes would raise the heights of the tracer by
   <Metric name="unclippedShift" />.
4. The height. $h_Z$ is the windowed scale height of the heights $|z|$ of the particles, each
   weighted by its initial mass, in the self-consistent window $L = 2h_Z$
   ([The window of the fit](./the-window)). The weight is the initial mass because the number of
   giants of an age, which the counts of the Milky Way use, is proportional to the initial mass of
   the population that made them.
5. The predictions. The tracer is $\exp(-\Delta\Phi/\sigma_z^2)$ in the fitted potential at
   $R_\mathrm{mid}$ ([The fitted potential](./the-potential)). The vertical Jeans equation uses the
   unclipped dispersion measured in slices of $|z|$ up to 12 kpc, without and with the tilt term.
   The sheet is $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ with $z_0 = \sigma_z^2/(\pi G\Sigma)$ and $\Sigma$ the mass
   per area within $|z| < 1.1$ kpc. The profiles of the tracer and of the Jeans equation go through
   the same window as the particles, so a predicted and a measured height are fitted the same way.
   The sheet needs no fit: the window returns $z_0$ for a $\mathrm{sech}^2$ profile within 0.3
   percent ([The window of the fit](./the-window)), so its predicted height is $z_0$ itself.
6. The errors. Every quantity is measured again on 300 bootstrap resamplings of the particles of
   the bin, so that a ratio of a predicted to a measured height carries the correlation of the two.

## Reading the fits

A profile drawn with a logarithmic density turns an exponential into a straight line, so the
departures of a population from an exponential show as curvature. The fit itself is the
exponential of scale $h_Z$ inside the window $L = 2h_Z$, normalized to the mass of the particles in
the window. The shading marks $L$ and $L/2$, the two windows whose fits are averaged.

The row of three examples shows the youngest population of the halo, one of 4-5 Gyr and the oldest,
9-10 Gyr. In Au24 they have $\sigma_z$ of 15.0, 35.1 and 89.9 km/s and $h_Z$ of 0.52, 1.19 and 3.26
kpc. Where a profile lies below its fitted exponential close to the plane, it is flatter than an
exponential there, as a population in the harmonic core of its potential is. That core has a fixed
height, about 0.4 kpc in the toy disk of [Where the test bites](./where-the-test-bites), so it
takes a larger part of the window of a cold population than of a hot one. The larger panel shows one population with the three predictions over its particles, each of unit mass
below 12 kpc, and the search for its window: $h(L)$ at the 40 trial windows, the line $L = 2h$,
and the window where they first meet.
