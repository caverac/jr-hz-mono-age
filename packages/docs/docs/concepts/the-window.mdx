# The window of the fit

A scale height is the scale of an exponential, but the profiles of the populations are not
exponentials: they flatten near the plane and fall more steeply above. The height that a fit
returns then depends on how far above the plane it looks. This page explains the fit that the
analysis uses (`experiments.heights`), why it looks inside a window $L$ that grows with the
population, and how the window is chosen.

## An exponential cut at a window

Fit the heights $|z|$ of a population, or a profile $\rho(z)$, inside a window $0 \le |z| < L$
with an exponential of scale $h$ cut at $L$:

$$
p(z) = \frac{e^{-z/h}}{h\,(1 - e^{-L/h})}, \qquad 0 \le z < L .
$$

Its mean height is

$$
\langle z \rangle = h - \frac{L}{e^{L/h} - 1},
$$

which runs from 0, for a scale much shorter than the window, to $L/2$, for a scale much longer,
when the exponential is flat across the window. The maximum-likelihood estimate of the rate
$1/h$ from a set of heights matches this mean to the mean of the data inside the window (Deemer &
Votaw 1955, Sect. 3), so the fit is the root of one equation in $h$ (`truncated_exp_scale`). The
root exists only while the mean of the data lies below $L/2$: a profile that is flat inside the
window has no scale there, and the fit gives none.

The analysis fits inside $L$ and inside $L/2$, and averages the two scales, as Palicio et al.
(2025) do (`particle_hz` for star particles, `profile_hz` for a predicted profile). For an
exponential both fits return its scale. For any other profile they differ, because the inner
window sees more of the flat core and the outer one more of the tail.

## Which window

A profile that is not exponential has no single scale, so its fitted $h$ depends on $L$:

- In a window much narrower than the profile, the fit sees the flat core and returns a large
  scale, or none when the mean reaches $L/2$.
- In a window much wider than the profile, the fit matches the mean height of the whole profile.
  For a $\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ layer that is $z_0 \ln 2$, whereas its exponential tail has a
  scale of $z_0/2$.

A window of fixed size, such as the $Z_\mathrm{lim}$ of 0.5-2.5 kpc that Palicio et al. (2025,
Table 1) choose for each galaxy, therefore measures thin and thick populations over different
parts of their profiles. The analysis lets the window grow with the population instead,
$L = k\,h(L)$ with $k = 2$ (`K_MAIN`), so that two populations of the same shape are always fitted
over the same part of their profiles. The equation $L = 2h(L)$ is solved on the 40 trial windows
of `WINDOW_GRID`, from 0.05 to 12 kpc, taking the first window at which $L - 2h(L)$ turns
positive and refining it with 30 geometric bisections (`self_consistent_window`). For a
$\mathrm{sech}^2$ layer the root returns $z_0$ within 0.3 percent, so the sheet and its scale
height are the same prediction.

The smallest root is the one taken. For a hot population with few stars inside a small window,
the profile is nearly flat there, and the noise of the fitted $1/h$ can satisfy $L = 2h(L)$ by
chance far below the true scale. A window therefore needs at least 100 star particles inside
$L/2$ (`N_MIN_HALF_WINDOW`). In 3000 draws of 300 heights from an exponential of $h = 3$ kpc, a
minimum of 20 gave a root below 1 kpc in 25 draws, a minimum of 50 in 1 and a minimum of 100 in
none.

What to look for:

1. The exponential has $h(L) = 0.4$ kpc at every window, so any window measures it. This is the
   only profile for which that holds.
2. The $\mathrm{sech}^2$ layer has a large $h(L)$ in small windows, which see its flat core: 2.0
   kpc in a window of 0.5 kpc. In large windows it approaches 0.555 kpc, $z_0 \ln 2$, the mean
   height of the whole profile. The self-consistent window, $L = 1.6$ kpc, returns 0.80 kpc, its
   $z_0$, whereas a fixed window of 1 kpc returns 1.10 kpc.
3. The mixture has no plateau: $h(L)$ rises from 0.29 kpc in a window of 0.2 kpc to 0.55 kpc in
   one of 12 kpc, as wider windows see more of the thicker component. The self-consistent window
   returns 0.31 kpc in $L = 0.61$ kpc.
4. Switching the density axis to logarithmic turns each fitted exponential into a straight line,
   and shows where the profile bends away from it.
