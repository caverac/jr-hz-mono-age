# Where the test bites

The tracer predicts the thickness of a population from its dispersion near the plane, under
the assumption that the population is isothermal. For a population that stays close to the
plane, part of that prediction holds whatever the shape of its distribution function, so its
agreement with the tracer says less than it seems. This page shows which part is guaranteed,
which part is not, and why the hot populations, which climb high above the plane, are where a
comparison with the tracer can fail.

## The vertical virial theorem

A steady population obeys the vertical Jeans equation. Without the tilt term
([The vertical Jeans equation](../status/tracer-vs-sheet#the-vertical-jeans-equation)) it reads

$$
\frac{d\left(\rho \langle v_z^2 \rangle\right)}{dz} = -\rho\, \frac{d\Delta\Phi}{dz}.
$$

Multiplying by $z$ and integrating over all heights, the left side becomes, after an
integration by parts, $-\int \rho \langle v_z^2\rangle\, dz$, because $z\rho\langle v_z^2\rangle$
vanishes far from the plane. Dividing by the mass of the population,

$$
\overline{v_z^2} = \overline{z\, \frac{d\Delta\Phi}{dz}},
$$

where the bars average over all the stars of the population. The kinetic energy of the vertical
motions balances the virial of the vertical force. Nothing in this step assumes a shape for the
distribution function.

## Inside the harmonic core

Near the plane of any smooth distribution of mass, the potential is harmonic,
$\Delta\Phi = \frac{1}{2}\Omega_z^2 z^2$. With a flat rotation curve, the Poisson equation gives
$\Omega_z^2 = 4\pi G \rho_0$ from the density $\rho_0$ at the plane. Then
$z\, d\Delta\Phi/dz = \Omega_z^2 z^2$, and the theorem becomes

$$
\overline{v_z^2} = \Omega_z^2\, \overline{z^2}.
$$

The rms height of the population is its rms velocity divided by $\Omega_z$, whatever the shape
of its distribution function. An isothermal tracer in the same potential is a Gaussian with the
same rms height, $\sigma_z/\Omega_z$. A population that stays inside the harmonic core therefore
has the rms height of the tracer of its rms velocity, isothermal or not.

Two conditions limit what this guarantees. The tracer takes $\sigma_z$ from the stars near the
plane, which is the rms velocity of the whole population only if the dispersion does not change
with height. In Auriga it rises with height in <Metric name="sigmaRisePopulations" />
populations. And the paper measures a scale height in a window, not an rms height. The window
responds to the shape of the profile as well as to its spread, so two profiles with the same rms
height can have different $h_Z$ (the chart below measures how different).

## Beyond the core

Above the core, $z\, d\Delta\Phi/dz$ is no longer $\Omega_z^2 z^2$, and its average depends on
how the stars spread over heights, not only on $\overline{z^2}$. Two populations with the same
$\overline{v_z^2}$ then have different rms heights: one with heavier tails reaches higher in a
potential that steepens more slowly than the harmonic one, and one with a sharper cutoff stays
lower. The shape of the distribution function sets the profile, whereas the tracer assumes one
particular shape. A match between the tracer and a hot population is a test of that shape in the potential
of the galaxy.

## Try it

The chart compares three distribution functions of the form $\exp[-(E_z/E_0)^q]$: $q = 1$ is
the isothermal tracer, $q = 0.7$ has heavier tails and $q = 1.5$ a sharper cutoff. Each $E_0$ is
chosen so that the population-averaged $\overline{v_z^2}$ is $\sigma_z^2$, the condition of the
virial theorem. The populations sit either in the toy disk of
[Tracer and sheet](./tracer-and-sheet) or in the harmonic potential of its midplane density,
0.075 $M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$.

The upper panel shows $\Delta\Phi/\sigma_z^2$ of both potentials. The toy disk stays within 10
percent of its harmonic core up to 0.40 kpc. Where $\Delta\Phi < 2\sigma_z^2$ an isothermal
population keeps all but $e^{-2}$ of its density. The middle panel shows the three profiles, each
of unit mass. The lower panel compares their heights with the isothermal one over a range of
$\sigma_z$, as rms heights or as the windowed $h_Z$ of the paper.

What the chart shows:

1. In the harmonic potential the rms heights of the three populations are equal at every
   $\sigma_z$, to the third decimal: the dotted lines lie on 1. This is the virial theorem.
2. In the toy disk the rms heights agree at $\sigma_z = 6$ km/s and drift apart as the
   populations leave the core, by 2-3 percent at 25 km/s, small because the rms height averages
   over the whole profile.
3. The windowed $h_Z$ differs between the shapes even in the harmonic potential: the heavier
   tails give 0.85 times the isothermal $h_Z$ and the sharper cutoff 1.14 times, at every
   $\sigma_z$, because a harmonic potential scales every population by the same factor. In the
   toy disk the difference grows with $\sigma_z$, to 0.80 and 1.19 at 60 km/s.

The windowed scale height is thus sensitive to the shape of a population at every dispersion,
and the potential adds to that sensitivity only once the population leaves the core. The
analysis reports the cold and the hot populations separately: below $h_Z = 1$ kpc the tracer
predicts <Metric name="coldTracer" /> within 10 percent, with a scatter of
<Metric name="coldTracerRms" />, and above 1.5 kpc <Metric name="hotTracer" />
([Tracer or sheet](../status/tracer-vs-sheet)).
