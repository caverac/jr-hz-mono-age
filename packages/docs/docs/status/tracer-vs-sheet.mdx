# Tracer or sheet

In the seven Auriga halos, the isothermal tracer in a fitted potential predicts the scale
height of <Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> of the <Metric name="populations" /> mono-age
populations within 10 percent, with a median ratio of <Metric name="tracerMedian" />, and
reproduces how $h_Z$, and hence the slope $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/h_Z$, changes with age in
every halo. The heights grow with $\sigma_z$ as fast as the tracer of each halo's potential
grows, within two standard errors in <Metric name="etaChordWithin2" /> halos. Over 2-10 Gyr
the tracer falls slightly short of the oldest populations, because the dispersion of a
population grows with height, even within 0.25 Gyr of ages. The vertical Jeans equation,
with the dispersion of each population measured at every height, removes that residual,
with a tilt term that holds up the thick old populations of Au19. The self-gravitating sheet,
the vertical half of the slope that the
[companion paper](https://github.com/caverac/jr-hz-relation) derives for a whole disk, does
not reproduce the heights of populations of one age, because it treats each population as
if it made the potential of the whole layer ([The sheet](#the-sheet)).

## Measurement

For each halo, the analysis fits an axisymmetric potential to all the particles of the
snapshot with AGAMA (Vasiliev 2019), as a CylSpline expansion for the gas and the stars of
the disk region and a Multipole expansion for everything else (`experiments.potential`).
The star particles of the annulus $7.5 < R < 9$ kpc are split into ten bins of 1 Gyr in
age. In each bin, $\sigma_z$ is the dispersion of $v_z$ of the stars with $|z| < 0.3$ kpc,
clipped at 4 standard deviations estimated from the median absolute deviation.
$J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$ is the median radial action of the stars with $|z| < 0.5$ kpc,
computed in the fitted potential with the Staeckel fudge of AGAMA
(`experiments.populations`).

We fit $h_Z$ to the heights $|z|$ of the stars, weighted by their initial mass, by matching
their mean height inside a window $|z| < L$ to that of an exponential truncated at $L$. For
an exponential profile, $1/h_Z$ is then the maximum-likelihood estimate of its rate, which
exists only when the mean height lies below $L/2$ (Deemer & Votaw 1955, Sect. 3), so a
window whose mean reaches $L/2$ gives no $h_Z$. The estimate is averaged with the same fit
inside $L/2$. The window scales with the population, $L = k\,h_Z$ with $k = 2$, taking the
smallest $L$ that satisfies the condition (`experiments.heights`), because the height of a
profile that is not exponential depends on the window
([Fixed-window bias](./window-bias)). A window needs 100 stars inside $L/2$. With fewer,
the profile of a hot population is nearly flat inside a small window, so the noise of the
fitted $1/h_Z$ can satisfy the condition by chance. With a minimum of 20, about one in a
hundred draws of 300 stars from an exponential of $h_Z = 3$ kpc gave such a spurious root
far below the true scale, whereas 100 gave none in 3000. Errors are half the range
between the 16th and the 84th percentiles of 300 bootstrap resamplings of the stars of each
bin. Each resampling recomputes every quantity at once, so that ratios of quantities
measured on the same stars, such as $a$ and the ratio of a predicted to the measured
$h_Z$, carry their correlations.

The tracer is the profile $\exp(-\Delta\Phi/\sigma_z^2)$, with $\Delta\Phi$ from the fitted
potential at the median radius of the annulus. It passes through the same self-consistent
window as the stars, so that the measured and the predicted $h_Z$ come from one estimator.
A second tracer uses the potential of the snapshot, averaged over azimuth, in place of the
fit, as a check of the fit. The sheet, which [The sheet](#the-sheet) compares with the
tracer, predicts $h_Z = \sigma_z^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$, the vertical half of the slope of the
companion paper, with $\Sigma$ the mass per area of all the particles of the annulus with
$|z| < 1.1$ kpc. We write $h_\mathrm{pred}$ for the height that any of these predictions, or
the vertical Jeans equation below, gives a population from its own $\sigma_z$. Because
$J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$ is measured for each population, the ratio of a predicted to the
measured $h_Z$ tests the vertical half of the slope alone.

## Age dependence

We fit $\beta_a$, the exponent of $a \propto \tau^{\beta_a}$, by weighted least squares of
$\ln a$ against $\ln \tau$ over the bins of 0-10 Gyr and of 2-10 Gyr, for the measured $a$
and for $a$ with each predicted $h_Z$ in place of the measured one. These exponents
describe how $a$ changes with age, but the difference between two of them is not a test
of a prediction. Each fit is weighted by its own errors, and $\ln a$ is not a straight line
in $\ln \tau$: the measured $\beta_a$ changes by <Metric name="exponentShift" /> when the
bins of 0-2 Gyr are left out. Two fits with different weights can therefore differ even
where the measured and the predicted $a$ agree bin by bin. The two values of $a$ also share
$J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$, whose error would enter twice in their difference.

The test is instead the trend of the ratio $h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z$, the exponent $p$ of
$h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z \propto \tau^p$ fitted by weighted least squares over the same bins,
with the bootstrap errors of the ratios as weights (`experiments.populations`).
$J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$ cancels in the ratio, and the fit uses one set of weights. With those
weights, the trend equals the measured minus the predicted $\beta_a$, so a prediction that
reproduces how $a$ changes with age has a trend of 0. Where the ratios scatter about a power
law by more than their errors, the error of the trend is multiplied by $\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$,
the root of the $\chi^2$ per degree of freedom of the fit, so that the scatter widens the
error instead of sharpening the test.

## The vertical Jeans equation

The residual of the tracer over 2-10 Gyr comes from its two simplifying assumptions, which
the vertical Jeans equation lets us drop. The tracer solves that equation for a steady,
axisymmetric population,

$$
\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial (R\nu\sigma_{Rz})}{\partial R} + \frac{\partial (\nu\sigma_z^2)}{\partial z}
= -\nu\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial z},
$$

under two assumptions: $\sigma_z$ does not change with height, and the first term, the tilt
term, vanishes. A population of 1 Gyr of ages mixes stars of several dispersions, so its
$\sigma_z$ can grow with height and its velocities can have heavier tails than one
Gaussian. We measure, in slices of $|z|$ up to 12 kpc, the variance of $v_z$ of each
population weighted by initial mass, without clipping, because the equation needs the
second moment of all its stars. The slices reach that high because the self-consistent
windows of the oldest populations of Au19 reach 8 kpc, and the equation holds $\sigma_z^2$
and the tilt term constant above the last slice.

The tilt term needs the radial derivative of $\nu\sigma_{Rz}$, which the 1.5 kpc of the
annulus measure poorly. We take it from the stars of each population over
$6 < R < 10.5$ kpc, a range centered on the annulus, in rings of 0.5 kpc. In each slice,
straight lines of $\ln\nu$ and of $\sigma_{Rz} = \langle v_R v_z\,\mathrm{sign}(z)\rangle$
against $R$ give

$$
t = \frac{1}{R\nu}\frac{\partial (R\nu\sigma_{Rz})}{\partial R}
= \sigma_{Rz}\left(\frac{1}{R} + \frac{\partial \ln\nu}{\partial R}\right) + \frac{\partial \sigma_{Rz}}{\partial R}
$$

at $R_\mathrm{mid}$, where the sign of $z$ folds the two sides of the plane, on which
$\sigma_{Rz}$ has opposite signs. A slice needs 50 stars in every ring, so that the lines
sample $R_\mathrm{mid}$ symmetrically. A slice without them counts its tilt term as 0.
With the tilt
term written as $\nu t(z)$, the equation integrates to

$$
\nu(z) = \frac{\sigma_z^2(0)}{\sigma_z^2(z)}
\exp\left[-\int_0^z \frac{\partial_{z'}\Phi + t}{\sigma_z^2}\,dz'\right],
$$

which goes through the same self-consistent window as the stars, without and with $t$
(`experiments.jeans`). The profile is a diagnostic of the tracer's assumptions, not a
prediction, because it uses the kinematics measured at every height. The same two
refinements are known from the inverse problem of measuring the local density with one
tracer in an N-body disk, where an isothermal tracer biases the result and its measured
dispersion profile removes the bias (Garbari, Read & Lake 2011), and where the tilt term is
needed for an unbiased estimate (Sivertsson et al. 2022). Figure 5 shows both
profiles with the buttons "Jeans, $\sigma_z(z)$" and "Jeans, $\sigma_z(z)$ and tilt".

## Half-mass heights

The $h_Z$ of a population depends on the window that measures it, whereas the height below
which half of its mass lies needs none. We measure that half-mass height for the stars of
each population with $|z| < 12$ kpc, weighted by initial mass, and for each predicted profile
over the same heights (`experiments.heights.half_mass_height`), with errors from the same
300 bootstrap resamplings. The ratio of a predicted to the measured half-mass height thus
tests each prediction over the whole profile, including the heights above $2h_Z$ that the
window leaves out.

## Disk stars, finer bins and clipping

Three checks ask whether the residual of the tracer over 2-10 Gyr comes from how the
populations are selected and measured (`experiments.robustness`). Each repeats the
measurement of $\sigma_z$, $h_Z$ and the tracer in its own bins, with 100 bootstrap
resamplings of its own, so that the main measurement does not change.

The annulus holds stars on orbits of every circularity $L_z/L_c(E)$, the angular momentum of
a star over that of the circular orbit of the same energy in the fitted potential. Keeping
only the stars above 0.7, on orbits of a disk, keeps <Metric name="diskMassRange" /> of the
mass of a bin, the least in the oldest bins. With these stars alone, the tracer predicts
$h_Z$ within 10 percent in <Metric name="diskWithin10" /> bins, its trends over 0-10 Gyr lie
within <Metric name="diskTrendMax0" /> standard errors of 0, and over 2-10 Gyr
<Metric name="diskTrendNegative2" /> are negative. The residual is therefore a property of the
disk stars, not of the stars on orbits closer to a spheroid's that a bin of age also holds.

Bins of 0.25 Gyr hold a quarter of the ages of the main bins. The tracer predicts $h_Z$
within 10 percent in <Metric name="fineWithin10" /> of them, a smaller share because each
bin holds fewer stars, and over 2-10 Gyr <Metric name="fineTrendNegative2" /> of its trends
are negative, up to <Metric name="fineTrendMax2" /> standard errors from 0. The residual thus
does not come from the mixing of ages inside a bin of 1 Gyr. A population of 0.25 Gyr of
ages is not one isothermal component either.

Without clipping, $\sigma_z$ keeps the tails of $v_z$, which raise the tracer heights by
<Metric name="unclippedShift" />. The tracer of the unclipped $\sigma_z$ predicts $h_Z$
within 10 percent in <Metric name="unclippedWithin10" /> bins, with a median ratio of
<Metric name="unclippedMedian" />, and its trends over 2-10 Gyr are negative in
<Metric name="unclippedTrendNegative2" /> halos. The clipping therefore does not cause the
residual, and the clipped dispersion predicts the heights better.

## The shape of the vertical actions

Ting & Rix (2026) describe a coeval population by the distribution of its vertical actions,
$p(J_z) \propto \exp[-(J_z/J_0)^{2-b}]$, which diffusion with a coefficient $\propto J_z^b$
leaves behind. Scatterers that fill the volume, such as bending waves or dark substructure,
give $b = 1$, whereas scatterers confined to a thin layer at the midplane, such as giant
molecular clouds, give $b = 1/2$, close to the $b = 0.51$ they measure for red-clump stars of
2-8 Gyr in the Milky Way. We fit $b$ and $J_0$ to the vertical actions of the stars of each
bin by maximum likelihood, weighted by initial mass, with the actions from the Staeckel fudge
of AGAMA in the fitted potential (`experiments.coeval`). An isothermal population is
exponential in $J_z$ only where the potential is harmonic: in a potential $\propto |z|^p$ its
energy grows as $J_z^{2p/(p+2)}$, so a linear potential gives it $b = 4/3$. We therefore read
each fitted $b$ against $b_\mathrm{iso}$, the $b$ of the isothermal tracer of the bin's
$\sigma_z$ in the same potential. Both need the orbits of the population to stay inside the
12 kpc of the grid of heights, which those of <Metric name="coevalCount" /> populations do.

The fitted $b$ is <Metric name="bFitRange" />, with errors of at most
<Metric name="bFitErrRange" />, and exceeds $b_\mathrm{iso}$ in <Metric name="bAboveIso" />
populations, by a median of <Metric name="bExcessMedian" />. The cold populations have $b$ of
<Metric name="bColdRange" />, against $b_\mathrm{iso}$ of <Metric name="bIsoColdRange" />.
The vertical actions of the Auriga populations thus have heavier tails than an isothermal
population of the same midplane dispersion, as their half-mass heights showed, and depart
from it in the direction opposite to the thin layer of Ting & Rix. The population with
$b = 1/2$ and the $\sigma_z$ of each bin, in the fitted potential, makes the same point
through the heights. With $J_0$ set so that its stars within 0.3 kpc of the plane have that
$\sigma_z$, its flat-topped velocities make it thicker than the tracer near the plane. It
predicts
$h_Z$ within 10 percent in <Metric name="coevalWithin10" /> populations, with a median ratio of
<Metric name="coevalMedian" /> and a scatter of <Metric name="coevalRms" />, whereas over the
same populations the tracer does so in <Metric name="coevalTracerWithin10" />, with a median of
<Metric name="coevalTracerMedian" />. The Auriga
populations are therefore not thin-layer populations, which is not surprising in hindsight:
the star-forming gas of these simulations follows an effective equation of state rather than
forming giant molecular clouds (Grand et al. 2017).

## The steady state

The tracer and the Jeans equation both assume a steady disk that is symmetric about its
plane. Each halo departs from that state, but the old populations of Au19, whose thickness
the tilt term explains, are no more disturbed than the populations of the other halos. We
measure four departures in the stars of each population inside its self-consistent window
(`experiments.equilibrium`): the thickening rate
$\langle \mathrm{sign}(z)\,v_z\rangle/\langle|z|\rangle$, which is
$d\ln\langle|z|\rangle/dt$ for stars that stay in the annulus, the bending velocity
$\langle v_z\rangle$, the north-south asymmetry of mass $(M_+ - M_-)/(M_+ + M_-)$, and the
amplitude of the $m = 1$ term of the mean $z$ over eight sectors of azimuth, a warp. All
four vanish in a steady disk that is axisymmetric and symmetric about its plane. Their
errors come from 300 bootstrap draws of the stars. The warp, a modulus, is positive even
without one, so its noise floor is about its error.

## The sheet

A self-gravitating isothermal sheet of surface density $\Sigma$ has the profile
$\mathrm{sech}^2(z/z_0)$ with $z_0 = \sigma_z^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$ (Spitzer 1942; van der Kruit
& Searle 1981), the vertical half of the slope that the companion paper derives for a whole
disk. Above $z_0$ the profile falls as an exponential of scale $z_0/2$, which is why papers
that use the exponential tail write $\sigma_z^2 = 2\pi G\Sigma h_z$. In the self-consistent
window, the estimator of the stars returns $z_0$ for the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ profile within
0.2 percent (`experiments.heights.sheet_profile`), so the sheet's $h_Z$ is $z_0$ measured as
the stars are, and the convention does not enter the comparison. Applied to one population,
the sheet appears in studies of disk heating and migration (Ro&scaron;kar et al. 2013;
Martig, Minchev & Flynn 2014b; Grand et al. 2016; Mackereth et al. 2019; Martig et al.
2026), most of which use it as a scaling rather than test it population by population. At
fixed $\Sigma$ it makes every population grow as $\sigma_z^2$, whereas the measured heights
grow as $\sigma_z^\eta$ with $\eta$ of <Metric name="etaRange" />. The sheet is the
isothermal tracer of a layer whose own mass makes its potential, the picture that the
companion paper adopts for a whole disk, whereas a population of 1 Gyr of ages is one part
of that layer and moves in the potential of all of it. Against the measured heights, the
sheet comes within 10 percent in <Metric name="sheetWithin10" /> populations, with ratios
from <Metric name="sheetRange" />: <Metric name="sheetYoung" /> for the youngest population
of each halo and <Metric name="sheetOld" /> for the oldest. Its trends with age over 0-10
Gyr are <Metric name="sheetTrendRange" />, at least <Metric name="sheetTrendMin" /> standard
errors from 0 in every halo, which is the difference between $\eta$ and 2 measured by a fit
(Figures 5, 7 and 8). The sheet thus fits a population only where $\sigma_z^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$
happens to cross its height, at intermediate ages, and departs from it on either side.

## Limitations

The tracer takes $\sigma_z$ from the stars within 0.3 kpc of the plane and assumes that it
does not change with height, an assumption whose cost the Jeans heights above measure. That
$\sigma_z$ is clipped at 4 standard deviations, which removes the tails of a population that
mixes several dispersions, so it is smaller than the second moment of the population, by an
amount whose effect on the heights the unclipped check above measures. The tracer also takes
$\Delta\Phi$ at a single radius, which neglects the radial terms of the vertical Jeans
equation, the tilt term that the Jeans heights above measure. That tilt term comes from
straight lines over 4.5 kpc of radius, which cannot follow a curvature of $\nu\sigma_{Rz}$
with radius, a limitation that its bootstrap errors do not include.

The potential is an axisymmetric fit, which cannot follow a bar, although averaged over
azimuth the snapshot's potential agrees with it
([overview](./overview#the-analysis-recovers-a-known-galaxy)). The bootstrap errors of the
predictions carry those of $\sigma_z$, but not those of the fitted potential, whose accuracy
we know only on the mock galaxy of the overview. The trend tests a power law in age, so a
prediction that fails in a few bins without a trend, as a bump in the ratios would, shows
up in $\chi^2_\nu$ rather than in the trend. Finally, every population comes from one
annulus per halo, so these results say nothing yet about how $\eta$ depends on radius.
