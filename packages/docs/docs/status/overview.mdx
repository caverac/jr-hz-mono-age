# Overview

We test whether the mono-age populations of a disk galaxy are isothermal tracers of the
total gravitational potential. A population of one age holds a small part of the mass of
its disk, so its vertical profile should be set by the whole potential it moves in, as
$\rho \propto \exp(-\Delta\Phi/\sigma_z^2)$, rather than by its own gravity, as in a
self-gravitating sheet. In the seven Auriga halos of Palicio et al. (2025), the tracer
predicts the scale height $h_Z$ of <Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> of the
<Metric name="populations" /> mono-age populations within 10 percent and reproduces how
$h_Z$ changes with age in every halo. Star counts of Gaia giants in the solar
neighborhood, split into populations by the ages of LAMOST and APOGEE, give the same answer
for the Milky Way. Summed over the populations of
each disk, the tracers also predict the thickness of the whole disk, and with it the heights
of the relation between radial action and scale height that Palicio et al. (2025) find
across galaxies. The one open question concerns the published heights of Imig et al.
(2025), which grow more slowly with age than our star counts of the same stars, for reasons
that our data cannot reach.

Every number on these pages is computed in the browser from the records that the Python
package writes under `assets/data/`, so the prose cannot drift from the data. A result that
the package has not produced is not quoted, whatever other evidence for it may exist. Where
a check was made only with code outside the package, the text says so and gives no number.

## The claim

The paper argues that a mono-age population of a disk behaves as an isothermal tracer of
the total gravitational potential, with the vertical density

$$
\rho(z) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{\Delta\Phi(R, z)}{\sigma_z^2}\right], \qquad
\Delta\Phi(R, z) = \Phi(R, z) - \Phi(R, 0),
$$

rather than as a self-gravitating isothermal sheet of surface density $\Sigma$, whose scale
height is $h_Z = \sigma_z^2/(\pi G \Sigma)$. At fixed $\Sigma$, the sheet makes every
population follow $h_Z \propto \sigma_z^2$. A tracer follows $h_Z \propto \sigma_z^\eta$
instead, where $\eta$ depends on the shape of $\Delta\Phi$ over the heights that the
population reaches: $\eta = 1$ where $\Delta\Phi \propto z^2$, as near the midplane of any
smooth mass distribution, and $\eta = 2$ where $\Delta\Phi \propto |z|$, as above a thin
one. Because the slope $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/h_Z$ of the $J_R$-$h_Z$ relation (Palicio
et al. 2025) divides by $h_Z$, the two pictures also predict different age dependences of
$a$.

We write $h_\mathrm{pred}$ for the scale height that a model of the vertical structure
predicts for a population from its own $\sigma_z$, whether the tracer, the sheet or the
vertical Jeans equation. Each model gives a profile in $|z|$, which we fit with the same
estimator as the stars, so that $h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z$ compares two heights measured the same
way. None of the predictions uses the $J_R$-$h_Z$ relation, whose slope $a$ we measure
population by population rather than assume.

## Results

### Auriga populations are isothermal tracers

We split the star particles of the annulus $7.5 < R < 9$ kpc of the seven halos of Table 1
of Palicio et al. (2025) at $z = 0$ into ten bins of 1 Gyr in age
([why these seven](../explorations/rendered-disks#why-these-seven-halos)), and fit $h_Z$ of
each in a window that scales with the population, $|z| < 2h_Z$. The tracer, in a potential
fitted to all the particles of each snapshot, predicts $h_Z$ within 5 percent in
<Metric name="tracerWithin5" />, within 10 percent in <Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> and
within 15 percent in <Metric name="tracerWithin15" /> of the
<Metric name="populations" /> populations, with ratios from
<Metric name="tracerRange" /> and a median of <Metric name="tracerMedian" /> (Figure 1).

The measured heights grow as $\sigma_z^\eta$ with $\eta$ of <Metric name="etaRange" />, in

The tracer also reproduces how the slope $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/h_Z$ changes with age. We
test this with the trend of $h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z$ with age, in which $J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$
cancels and which equals the measured minus the predicted age exponent of $a$. Over
0-10 Gyr, the trend of the tracer lies within <Metric name="trendMax0" /> standard errors of
0 in every halo, with values of <Metric name="trendRange0" />
([Tracer or sheet](./tracer-vs-sheet#age-dependence)).

A small residual remains over 2-10 Gyr, where the trend of the tracer is negative in
<Metric name="trendNegative2" /> halos, a split of signs with a probability of
<Metric name="trendSignTest2" /> under symmetric scatter. It comes from the isothermal
assumption itself, because a bin of 1 Gyr mixes stars of several dispersions, whose
$\sigma_z$ grows with height, whereas the tracer takes one clipped dispersion from the
plane. The vertical Jeans equation,
integrated with the full second moment of $v_z$ measured at every height, removes the
pattern, with <Metric name="jeansSigmaNegative2" /> trends over 2-10 Gyr negative and
<Metric name="jeansSigmaWithin2" /> of its 14 trends within 2 standard errors of 0. The old
populations of Au19 need one more term, the tilt term of the Jeans equation: their Jeans
heights are <Metric name="au19JeansOld" /> of the measured ones without it and
<Metric name="au19JeansTiltOld" /> with it. Their mean vertical motions, with
thickening rates of <Metric name="au19ThickeningOld" /> per Gyr, show no more departure from
a steady state than those of the other halos
([The vertical Jeans equation](./tracer-vs-sheet#the-vertical-jeans-equation)).

### The window of the fit

The scale height of a profile that is not exponential depends on the window of $|z|$ it is
fit in, which is why we fit every population in a window that scales with it. Injected
profiles of known shape show how much the choice matters. In
<Metric name="injectionDraws" /> draws of <Metric name="injectionStars" /> heights, a fixed
window recovers exponentials within <Metric name="exponentialFixedBias" /> of their scale,
as the maximum-likelihood estimate of an exponential should. It returns
<Metric name="sech2Thin" /> times the self-consistent $h_Z$ of a $\mathrm{sech}^2$ layer that
is thin against the window and <Metric name="sech2Window" /> times that of one as thick as
the window, whereas the self-consistent window recovers every profile within
<Metric name="selfConsistentBias" />. A profile that is flat near the plane has no single
scale, so a window narrower than the profile reads its flat core as a large scale. The
Auriga populations follow the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ curve: below $h_Z = 0.5\,Z_\mathrm{lim}$
their median ratio is <Metric name="windowLowHalos" />, against
<Metric name="windowLowSech2" /> for $\mathrm{sech}^2$ and
<Metric name="windowLowExponential" /> for an exponential, as expected of tracers in a
potential that is harmonic near the plane ([Fixed-window bias](./window-bias)). The
heights in the self-consistent window are therefore the ones the tracer is tested with,
here and in the comparison across galaxies below.

### The tracers predict the heights of the relation across galaxies

We have not found the cause in our data. Checks outside the package rule out the causes
that our data can reach: the young populations hold almost no stars above our counts and
are already thinner than their model below 0.6 kpc, the trend is the same on both sides of
our cylinder, and the giants of their selection differ from ours in dispersion by the same
few percent at every age. The different ranges of the two fits account for at most a fifth
of it, since the exponential fit to the whole profile of a tracer is
<MwMetric name="estimatorWhole" /> times the fit below 1.2 kpc. What remains presumably
lies in their global fit, which ties the heights at the Sun to the rest of the disk through
one exponential in $|z|$ with a linear flare, or in their selection function near the
plane, neither of which we can test without refitting their model
([How their heights grow with age](./milky-way#how-their-heights-grow-with-age)).

## Open questions

The heights of Imig et al. (2025) still grow more slowly with age and with $\sigma_z$ than
our counts, for reasons that our data cannot reach
([The heights of Imig et al.](#the-heights-of-imig-et-al)). Because the tracer agrees with
the counts, the claim holds in the Milky Way unless the counts, rather than their fit,
misplace how the heights grow with age.

Two parts of the picture lie outside these tests. Every Auriga population comes from one
annulus per halo, so we do not yet know how $\eta$ depends on radius. The tracer also says
nothing about the radial action: we measure the slope $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/h_Z$
population by population, and hence the ratio of radial to vertical heating, but do not
explain it.

## Conclusions

1. In the seven Auriga halos, the isothermal tracer predicts the scale height of
   <Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> of the <Metric name="populations" /> mono-age
   populations within 10 percent, because each population moves in the whole potential of
   its disk rather than in its own.
2. The tracer reproduces how $h_Z$, and hence the slope $a$ of the $J_R$-$h_Z$ relation,
   changes with age, within <Metric name="trendMax0" /> standard errors in every halo over
   0-10 Gyr. The residual over 2-10 Gyr comes from treating each population as a single
   isothermal component, and the tilt term holds up the old populations of Au19.
3. The populations are flat near the plane, so their heights depend on the window of the
   fit. The self-consistent window recovers every injected profile within
   <Metric name="selfConsistentBias" />.
4. Summed over rings and ages, the tracers predict <Metric name="sumMixtureScaled" /> of the
   whole-disk heights, and the slope across galaxies that follows from them,
   <JrMetric name="slopeScaledMixture" />, agrees within its error with that of Palicio et
   al. (2025).
5. In the Milky Way, the tracer predicts the star counts of Gaia giants on every age scale
   we tried, with no trend with age.
6. The heights of Imig et al. (2025) agree with our counts in level and in their dependence
   on metallicity once both are fit the same way, but grow more slowly with age and with
   $\sigma_z$, at a rate consistent with $h_Z \propto \sigma_z$, for reasons that lie beyond
   the reach of our data.
