# Overview

We test, population by population, whether the mono-age populations of a disk galaxy are
isothermal tracers of the total gravitational potential. The assumption is old: models of
the Milky Way disk have built it from isothermal populations of each age in the total
potential since Bienaym&eacute;, Robin & Cr&eacute;z&eacute; (1987), as in the
Besan&ccedil;on model (Robin et al. 2003) and the model of Just & Jahreiss (2010), with
dispersions taken from an assumed relation between age and velocity dispersion and with
predictions compared with star counts that mix all ages. The companion paper,
[`jr-hz-relation`](https://github.com/caverac/jr-hz-relation), derives the slope of the
relation between radial action and scale height that Palicio et al. (2025) find across
galaxies, treating a whole disk as a self-gravitating isothermal sheet. A population of one
age holds a small part of the mass of its disk, so its vertical profile should follow the
whole potential it moves in, as $\rho \propto \exp(-\Delta\Phi/\sigma_z^2)$, rather than
that of a sheet of its own. In the seven Auriga halos of Palicio et al. (2025), whose
heights are known independently, the tracer predicts the scale height $h_Z$ of
<Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> of the <Metric name="populations" /> mono-age populations
within 10 percent and reproduces how $h_Z$ changes with age in every halo. The heights grow
as $\sigma_z^\eta$ with $\eta$ of <Metric name="etaRange" />, so the sheet, which studies of
disk heating and migration apply to single populations, makes the young populations too
thin and the old ones too thick. Star counts of Gaia giants in the solar neighborhood, split
into populations by the ages of LAMOST and APOGEE, follow the tracer with no trend with age,
at <MwMetric name="tracerMedians" /> of the counts in the median in the potential of McMillan
(2017), and at <MwMetric name="familyTracerMedians" /> in potentials that match the measured
density near the Sun. Inverted, they give midplane densities of
<MwMetric name="inversionRho0" /> $M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$. Summed over the
populations of each disk, the tracers also predict the thickness of the whole disk. The one
open question concerns the published heights of Imig et al. (2025), which grow more slowly
with age than our star counts of the same stars, for reasons that our data cannot reach.

## The claim

The companion paper derives the slope of the $J_R$-$h_Z$ relation for a whole disk from the
radial action of an epicycle and the scale height of a self-gravitating isothermal sheet of
surface density $\Sigma$,

$$
J_R^{\max} = c_{\max}\frac{\sigma_R^2}{\kappa}, \qquad
h_Z = \frac{\sigma_z^2}{\pi G \Sigma},
$$

so that

$$
a = \frac{J_R^{\max}}{h_Z} = c_{\max}\,\frac{\pi G \Sigma}{\kappa}
\left(\frac{\sigma_R}{\sigma_z}\right)^2,
$$

with the factor of order unity between the scale of the sheet and a fitted $h_Z$ absorbed
into $c_{\max}$. The sheet is the isothermal tracer of the potential that its own mass
makes, the picture that the companion paper adopts for a whole disk. A population of one
age holds only part of that mass, so we keep the radial half of the derivation and test its
vertical half population by population. We argue that such a population behaves as an
isothermal tracer of the total gravitational potential, with the vertical density

$$
\rho(z) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{\Delta\Phi(R, z)}{\sigma_z^2}\right], \qquad
\Delta\Phi(R, z) = \Phi(R, z) - \Phi(R, 0).
$$

The tracer itself is not new. Models of the Milky Way disk assume it for every population of
one age (Bienaym&eacute; et al. 1987; Robin et al. 2003; Just & Jahreiss 2010; Sysoliatina &
Just 2022), and studies of mono-abundance populations use it the other way round, fitting
the potential to populations whose heights are left free (Bovy et al. 2012; Bovy & Rix
2013). Predicting the density of one tracer from its kinematics in a potential goes back to
Bahcall (1984) and Holmberg & Flynn (2000), and Ludlow et al. (2021) predict the heights of
whole N-body disks from their dispersions. What these pages add is a test of the assumption
population by population, in cosmological disks whose heights are measured independently,
and a measurement of how the heights of mono-age populations scale with their dispersion.

Applied population by population with the $\Sigma$ of the whole disk, as in our tests, the
sheet makes every population follow $h_Z \propto \sigma_z^2$. A tracer follows
$h_Z \propto \sigma_z^\eta$ instead, where $\eta$ is the local slope of its height against
its dispersion, set by the shape of $\Delta\Phi$ over the heights that the population
reaches. For $\Delta\Phi \propto |z|^p$, $\eta = 2/p$: $\eta = 1$ where
$\Delta\Phi \propto z^2$, as near the midplane of any smooth mass distribution, and
$\eta = 2$ where $\Delta\Phi \propto |z|$, as above a thin one (van der Kruit 1988). Far
above the plane, where a halo dominates, $\Delta\Phi$ grows more slowly than $|z|$, so
$\eta$ exceeds 2 and the profile has no exponential scale at all. Because the slope divides
by $h_Z$, the two pictures predict different age dependences of $a$. For dispersions that
grow with age as $\mathrm{age}^{\beta_R}$ and $\mathrm{age}^{\beta_z}$, at fixed $\Sigma$
and $\kappa$, the sheet gives $a \propto \mathrm{age}^{2(\beta_R - \beta_z)}$, whereas the
tracer gives $a \propto \mathrm{age}^{2\beta_R - \eta\beta_z}$ over a range of $\sigma_z$ in
which $\eta$ is constant.

We write $h_\mathrm{pred}$ for the scale height that a model of the vertical structure
predicts for a population from its own $\sigma_z$: the sheet, which is the vertical half of
the derivation above; the tracer, which replaces it; or the vertical Jeans equation. Each
model gives a profile in $|z|$, which we fit with the same estimator as the stars, so that
$h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z$ compares two heights measured the same way. For a population we write
the slope as $a = J_{R,\mathrm{med}}/h_Z$, with the median radial action of its stars.
Because we measure $J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$ rather than predict it, $h_\mathrm{pred}/h_Z$ tests
the vertical half of the slope alone.

### The window of the fit

The scale height of a profile that is not exponential depends on the window of $|z|$ it is
fit in, which is why we fit every population in a window that scales with it. Injected
profiles of known shape show how much the choice matters. In
<Metric name="injectionDraws" /> draws of <Metric name="injectionStars" /> heights, a fixed
window recovers exponentials within <Metric name="exponentialFixedBias" /> of their scale,
as the maximum-likelihood estimate of an exponential should. It returns
<Metric name="sech2Thin" /> times the self-consistent $h_Z$ of a $\mathrm{sech}^2$ layer that
is thin against the window and <Metric name="sech2Window" /> times that of one as thick as
the window, whereas the self-consistent window recovers every profile within
<Metric name="selfConsistentBias" />. A profile that is flat near the plane has no single
scale, so a window narrower than the profile reads its flat core as a large scale. The
Auriga populations follow the $\mathrm{sech}^2$ curve: below $h_Z = 0.5\,Z_\mathrm{lim}$
their median ratio is <Metric name="windowLowHalos" />, against
<Metric name="windowLowSech2" /> for $\mathrm{sech}^2$ and
<Metric name="windowLowExponential" /> for an exponential, as expected of tracers in a
potential that is harmonic near the plane ([Fixed-window bias](./window-bias)). The
heights in the self-consistent window are therefore the ones the tracer is tested with,
here and in the comparison across galaxies below.

alternately, run from metal-poor on the left to metal-rich on the right, around a thick
line that joins the medians of the bins._

We have not found the cause in our data. Checks outside the package rule out the causes
that our data can reach: the young populations hold almost no stars above our counts and
are already thinner than their model below 0.6 kpc, the trend is the same on both sides of
our cylinder, and the giants of their selection differ from ours in dispersion by the same
few percent at every age. The different ranges of the two fits account for at most a fifth
of it, since the exponential fit to the whole profile of a tracer is
<MwMetric name="estimatorWhole" /> times the fit below 1.2 kpc. What remains presumably
lies in their global fit, which ties the heights at the Sun to the rest of the disk through
one exponential in $|z|$ with a linear flare, or in their selection function near the
plane, neither of which we can test without refitting their model
([How their heights grow with age](./milky-way#how-their-heights-grow-with-age)).

## Open questions

The heights of Imig et al. (2025) still grow more slowly with age and with $\sigma_z$ than
our counts, for reasons that our data cannot reach
([The heights of Imig et al.](#the-heights-of-imig-et-al)). Because the tracer agrees with
the counts, the claim holds in the Milky Way unless the counts, rather than their fit,
misplace how the heights grow with age.

Whether a population of one age is isothermal is itself contested. Sharma et al. (2021)
find that the dispersions of stars of fixed age and metallicity rise with height. Ting & Rix
(2026) and Djuri&cacute; & Sch&ouml;nrich (2026) argue that a population born at once departs
from the isothermal shape toward a sharper cutoff in its vertical actions. The Auriga
populations depart in the other direction, with heavier tails in their actions and more mass
far from the plane than an isothermal profile at every age, as a dispersion that rises with
height gives. The star counts of the solar neighborhood do not separate the two shapes: the
thin layer fits them only in a potential denser near the Sun than measured, whereas the
isothermal tracer fits them at the measured density. The error of $\rho_0$ keeps that
preference weak.

The Auriga snapshots are at the standard resolution of the Auriga project, whose stellar
softening is comparable to the heights of the youngest populations. Pakmor et al. (2025)
find that the rise of the velocity dispersion at large radii seen at that resolution might
be entirely numerical, which the Level 3 runs of the release would test. Every Auriga
population also comes from one annulus per halo, so we do not yet know how $\eta$ depends on
radius. In the Milky Way, the inversion fixes $\rho_0$ but not $\Sigma_{1.1}$. The Jeans
equation there leaves out the tilt term, which our sample cannot measure.

We have also not tested the radial half of the derivation,
$J_R^{\max} = c_{\max}\sigma_R^2/\kappa$, on populations of one age: we measure
$J_{R,\mathrm{med}}$ of every population, and hence the ratio of radial to vertical heating
in $a$, but have not compared it with $\sigma_R^2/\kappa$.

## Conclusions

1. In the seven Auriga halos, the isothermal tracer predicts the scale height of
   <Metric name="tracerWithin10" /> of the <Metric name="populations" /> mono-age
   populations within 10 percent (<Metric name="diskTracer" /> of the disk populations),
   because each population moves in the whole potential of its disk rather than in its
   own. The Jeans equation with the measured $\sigma_z(z)$ and the tilt term, which holds
   for any steady population, sets the floor of the test at a scatter of
   <Metric name="jeansRms" />.
2. The heights grow as $\sigma_z^\eta$ with $\eta$ of <Metric name="etaRange" />, the mean
   local slope of the tracer of each halo's potential within two standard errors in
   <Metric name="etaChordWithin2" /> halos, so an age-thickness relation grows as
   $\mathrm{age}^{\eta\beta_z}$ rather than the $\mathrm{age}^{2\beta_z}$ of a
   self-gravitating sheet, which makes young populations too thin and old ones too thick.
3. The tracer reproduces how $h_Z$, and hence the slope $a$ of the $J_R$-$h_Z$ relation,
   changes with age, within <Metric name="trendMax0" /> standard errors in every halo over
   0-10 Gyr. The residual over 2-10 Gyr, which persists in bins of 0.25 Gyr and with the
   disk stars alone, comes from a dispersion that rises with height, which also puts more
   mass far from the plane than the tracer at every age and gives the vertical actions
   heavier tails than an isothermal population, the opposite of the thin layer of Ting & Rix
   (2026). The Jeans equation with the
   measured $\sigma_z(z)$ and the tilt term removes both, the tilt term holding up the old
   populations of Au19.
4. The populations are flat near the plane, so their heights depend on the window of the
   fit, as known for disks since van der Kruit & Searle (1981). The self-consistent window
   recovers every injected profile within <Metric name="selfConsistentBias" />.
5. Summed over rings and ages, the tracers predict <Metric name="sumMixtureScaled" /> of the
   whole-disk heights and follow the flare of every disk. The slope across galaxies that
   follows from them tracks the slope with the measured heights, but its agreement with
   that of Palicio et al. (2025) rests on <JrMetric name="influentialHalo" /> alone.
6. In the Milky Way, the tracer follows the star counts of Gaia giants with no trend with
   age on every age scale we tried, at <MwMetric name="tracerMedians" /> of the counts in
   the median in the potential of McMillan (2017), whose density near the Sun exceeds the
   measured one, and at <MwMetric name="familyTracerMedians" /> in potentials that match
   it. Inverted, the counts give midplane densities of <MwMetric name="inversionRho0" />
   $M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$. Doubling the variance of the age errors leaves the test in
   place.
7. The heights of Imig et al. (2025) agree with our counts in level and in their dependence
   on metallicity once both are fit the same way, but grow more slowly with age and with
   $\sigma_z$, at a rate consistent with $h_Z \propto \sigma_z$, for reasons that lie beyond
   the reach of our data.
